# Module 3, Video 2: Hardcore Trading Psychology

Welcome to video number two, part of Module 3 on psychology. At this stage I'd like to really focus on what I believe is the most crucial aspect of trading: your hardcore psychology. I'm going to talk about things that might sound super simple, even perhaps childish, but believe me — when you're confronted every day in your trading with money on the line, your personal psychology will play tricks on you, as we saw in the previous video. The investor psychological cycle is really intense, but it's up to us to understand it and control it as much as we can. As long as we can understand our inner thoughts and feelings, and be true to ourselves, we can start mastering this cycle of emotion and use it to our benefit.

Having the right psychology in trading involves two important steps.

## Step One: Accept Losses as Part of the Business

You have to accept the fact that, like most businesses, there can be collateral damage — it is never a 100% win situation. You need to work hard on your mental game and start to internally accept losses as part of your trading business. To do that, you need to detach yourself, and most importantly your ego, from your trading. And when I say accept losses, I really mean it — you must honestly and truthfully accept them from an intellectual, emotional, and psychological point of view. The only way to internalize this first step is by understanding the methodology and process you're going to work with, the odds associated with it, and the management of your risk — and adapting all three components to your own personality and psychology.

Obviously, nobody likes to take a loss. But some people take it personally, as a blow to their ego. When a trader is fighting the market — fighting the fact that he should cut his loss short — he's really fighting his own ego. Please leave your ego at the door (not the Lego). The ego is strong-willed, and it can make people do things they shouldn't do — most of the time, you will. And when it takes over the trader, he's prone to making irrational and emotional decisions.

Ego is part of everyday life — with your kids, your wife, your girlfriend, your boss — and suddenly you start feeling it knocking at the door, pumping itself up inside you. At that stage, if you let it take over, you're finished. You'll start doing irrational things, saying the wrong things, doing the wrong things. In your trading it won't be any different — it will be the exact same way you'd conduct yourself in every situation in everyday life where you have to control yourself. So you must be stronger and control your ego. Stick to your plan, exit the bad position, and forget everything, because each trade should be started mentally from scratch. Don't hold a grudge and don't take it personally, because losing trades are, again, part of your trading. One trade means absolutely nothing in the scheme of things, especially if you control your risk the way you should. If you can't control your ego, it will lead you to overtrading and over-leveraging.

Also, please don't beat yourself up. Figure out the markets or instruments you're good at — the type of setup that works for you — and try to get rid of the ones you're not good at. There's absolutely no need to prove your point to the market. Markets are totally impersonal and have nothing against you at all — they don't know you, they don't know your name, they don't know where you live, and they absolutely don't care. So be humble, move on, and concentrate on what you're good at.

There's a quote from Bruce Kovner, a very famous hedge fund manager also featured in Market Wizards: "If you personalize your losses, you can't trade." That beautiful sentence sums up everything I just told you. You have to absolutely separate yourself from your losses and accept them as part of your trading business.

## Step Two: Be Disciplined

This is the key — the absolute key — to your success. What do I mean by that?

**Wait for the right moment.** Wait for your signals as dictated by your methodology. Pay attention to your entry points; wait for your areas of support or resistance to be touched, as we looked at in previous videos; wait for a retracement; and do not give in to FOMO, the fear of missing out (we'll look at that a bit later). Do not chase the market — sit back, stay focused, and do nothing if you don't get your signals or the right opportunities. No trading is also part of trading; not having any positions will happen. The market may be dead or choppy — you just stay out, you don't do anything, and it doesn't matter. It's the same way that when we start heading into a bear market, the market becomes very dangerous, and it's better to be in cash — cash is king. So fight the urge to trade all the little counter-waves of the main trend; these are low-probability trades, and they'll distract you from your main objectives.

**Do not overtrade.** Stick to two or three trades a day, max — or if your style is more swing trading, stick to two or three trades a week. That's more than enough. You'll be able to make a lot of money with successful trades like that, without overtrading, rather than doing the opposite. And do not hold onto your losers — cut your losses short. For me, for example, my average win ratio oscillates between 70-75%, sometimes up to 80%, but my daily green days are more like 75-80%. That's only because I manage my losses better — more efficiently — than my wins. I really stick to cutting my losses short and try to hold my winners longer. This helps me stay in the game and helps me avoid burning my account.

Whatever your weaknesses are, only you can really see through them. After exchanging with you a few times, I'll probably be able to understand your weaknesses and help you, lead you, to overcome them and be disciplined about working on them. But you're the one who knows yourself better than anyone else — so please be true, look at yourself in the mirror, and don't escape reality. That's the most important thing: isolate your weaknesses — you know what they are — and work on them.

So you'll ask me, how do I work on my weaknesses? One thing you can do is start religiously keeping a trading journal and study it every day, or at the very least at the end of every single week (we'll talk more about this in Module 8). You will usually learn even more from your mistakes, from your bad trades, than from your good trades. The good trades you make, you're already good at — you're doing the right thing, you know you're on the right path, so there's not much harm in not studying them closely; they come naturally to you. But the real advantage lies in studying your bad trades, the mistakes you make — mistakes you might repeat again and again. Only by studying them religiously, every day or every week, will you be able to isolate these mistakes and avoid them. Just by avoiding these mistakes — not making these trades — you will already improve your trading by 100%. This is a lot of work, I agree, it's hard work. But like we say, no pain, no gain.

## Carrying Out the Steps: Practical Discipline

Knowing how to trade is one thing; actually being able to carry out the steps that make you a successful trader is another. So again, we're back to being extra disciplined. What do we do to be disciplined?

**First, do your homework.** **Second, get prepared pre-market open**, because when you do your analysis while the market is closed, you're not pressured by the market — you don't get market action or direction influencing you. So do your homework and get prepared before the market opens.

**Third, follow your method and trading plan.** We talked about this — please follow your own plan. Don't start comparing yourself to others; they're not paying your bills, so stick to your own plan.

I'd like to quote Mr. Kovner again: he says the first rule of trading — and probably one of the very first rules, and he's right — is "don't get caught in a situation in which you can lose a great deal of money for reasons that you don't understand." That's why we're talking about being prepared, having a plan, knowing what we're going to do. If we lose money, it's okay — it doesn't mean the trade was a bad trade. It could still be a good trade; it just means we had a plan, we had a strategy, we implemented it, and the market went the other way this time. It doesn't matter — it was still a good trade because we followed our process, our plan, and our strategy, and at least we knew what we were doing. The worst thing you could do, as Kovner implies, is go in unprepared — do stupid things and lose money without even understanding why you lost it. So again: preparation, homework, following the method, and not comparing yourself to anyone.

**Fourth, do not trade choppy markets.** Only trade markets showing momentum and trend. That brings me to the next point: do not trade against the trend. In my methodology I'm more of a trend follower than anything. We will also look at contrarian trading, which lets us take a position when a market might have gone really, really high or really, really low — though there's no such thing as "really high" or "really low," since really low for stocks is zero, and there's no ceiling on really high because it can go up to the roof. We will look at contrarian trading where we can use certain techniques to take advantage of excessively exuberant markets, but generally speaking, my style of trading is very much a trend-following one. I rarely trade against the trend — I wait for confirmation of the trend, and that's where I'll be coming in.

**Sixth point (very important): trade the market that you're in, and not the one that you want to be in.** It can be very expensive to try to convince the market that you're right. As someone once said — I forget the name now, but it might come back to me — the market can stay exuberant or irrational a lot longer than your account can withstand it. So again, trade the market you're in — look at what it's doing right now, not yesterday, and don't try to figure out tomorrow, just right now. Don't trade the market you'd like to be in, or that you'd like to imagine being in.

**Another rule: follow your risk management** — as if your life depended on it. Very important — that's also the key to success in trading and to being disciplined. Cut your losers short, as we've discussed, and let your winners run as much as you can psychologically take it — again, we go back to the psychological factor. Some people are very good at keeping a position for a long time and still watching it go against them, showing some drawdown, with the P&L moving every day. Some people have this old-fashioned way of hiding their P&L so they're not influenced by those dollar signs constantly ticking in front of them. But one thing for sure: try to be psychologically adapted to it — cut your losses short and let your winners run as much as you can psychologically take it.

Another point: remember, you're not here for the fun or excitement. We've seen that the fun gambler, or even to some extent the impulsive trader, tends to look at the market as a place to have fun. This is not a place to have fun — you're here to make money, simple as that. If you want to have fun, there are fun fairs, extreme sports, and casinos. You're here to make money and preserve the precious capital that you worked very hard for — at least for the majority of us. So treat your active investing like a real business.

And lastly, always remember: it's a marathon, not a sprint. It will take time. We haven't built the city of Rome in one day, nor two days, nor a week — everything takes time, little by little, step by step.
