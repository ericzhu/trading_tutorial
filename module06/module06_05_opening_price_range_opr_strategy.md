# Module 6, Video 5: The Opening Price Range (OPR) Breakout Strategy

This video covers the last major strategy that rounds out the core strategies and the MTF conversion system: the **Opening Price Range (OPR) breakout strategy**. (The video closes with the "Ten Commandments of Trading Academy," covered separately.)

## What Is the Opening Price Range (OPR)?

The opening range is simply the high and low of a given period right after the market opens. The market officially opens in New York at 9:30 a.m. Eastern Time, and the opening range is usually defined as the first 5, 10, or 15 minutes of trading — most pro traders use either the opening 1, 5, or 15 minutes.

### Why the Open Matters So Much
The market open is the meeting point of everyone who decided, for whatever reason, to trade a particular stock that morning — people who watched CNBC the night before, read the Wall Street Journal on their commute, institutional portfolio managers who spent days deliberating, investment committees that just approved a position, people who heard rumors at a dinner party or got a tip from their dentist or their broker. All of these very different people, with very different motivations, converge virtually in the same electronic marketplace at the same moment, transmitting buy/sell orders through their brokers alongside market makers and speculators reacting to pre-market action, news, etc.

Depending on the net balance of buying vs. selling power from this crowd, the stock opens up, down, or unchanged — but the first 5, 15, or 30 minutes function like a real "gladiator fight," with each side trying to impose its view on where the stock should go. This is why the opening range gives such a genuine read on what the market actually wants to do — as opposed to what we want it to do. We have our plan, our system, but ultimately the market does whatever it wants; the opening range is where we observe and listen before making our call.

The opening bell period is associated with the day's highest volume and volatility — typically the largest share of the day's total volume occurs here in about 90% of cases — which is why so many traders concentrate on this first hour. Many traders of different styles use the opening range both to set entry points and to forecast the day's price action.

Also account for **pre-market highs and lows** (roughly 2 hours before the 9:30 a.m. open) — these levels often act like a magnet on price action once the bell rings. Pre-market volume can vary a lot depending on news and how early players choose to get active, but it's generally much lighter than regular-hours volume, since most serious/professional players wait for the official open before committing.

## OPR Mechanics

### OPR 15-Minute Example
Take the first 15-minute candle of the trading day. Its high and low define the **OPR** for that day. If a subsequent 15-minute candle pierces through the high of the first candle, that's an **OPR 15-minute breakout** — the trigger to buy on the breakout of the OPR high.
- Stop: placed right below the low of the first (opening) candle.
- TP1: set at an equivalent distance to achieve the standard risk/reward ratio of 1.
- Depending on whether you trade intraday only or want to swing the position, adapt position management and TP rules accordingly — you can start with intraday rules and still choose to carry the trade overnight/over the weekend if it's proven itself in your favor.

The same logic applies for an opening gap down: breaking below the OPR low is the short trigger, with the stop placed above the high of the first candle.

### OPR 5-Minute Example
Identical logic, just using the first 5-minute candle's high/low as the OPR boundaries. A breakout of the OPR 5-minute high triggers a long entry, stop below the recent low, TP1 set to achieve a risk/reward ratio of 1.

## Process for Trading the OPR Breakout Strategy

Assume a scanner (covered in detail in Module 7) has flagged candidate stock XYZ as ticking all the boxes for a long setup. Steps to work through before pulling the trigger:

1. **Evaluate the historical context** — where is the stock trading, where are the majority of longs/shorts likely positioned, what's the current short float (a high short float can mean more short-covering fuel on the upside), what's the current upside/downside potential, and what risk/reward ratio is being offered (minimum of 1 to even consider the trade).
2. **Check for a news catalyst** — earnings/surprise earnings, a new product, an FDA drug approval, drug trial results, a new contract, CEO news, or anything else directly tied to the business that could pull investor/speculator attention to the stock that day.
3. **Check the size of the opening gap** — using pre-market quotes to spot the day's biggest movers immediately. Gaps above roughly **30%** call for extra caution: by the time the market opens, existing holders will often look to take partial profits, which can push the stock back down right on the open (this is exactly what a trader holding a full-size position would likely do too). Don't rush in on the open with a large percentage gap — wait for confirmation.
4. **Preference for gap size and confirmation timeframe** — generally prefer more modest gaps, roughly **5%–30%** up or down, and use confirmation from the **OPR 15-minute** breakout (more conservative but more reliable) rather than the 5-minute. For larger gaps (above 30%), it can make sense to wait only for the OPR 5-minute breakout confirmation instead. There's no fixed formula — it's a judgment call based on the combination of factors present, your assessment of expected volatility, and your gut feel for that specific day.
5. **Width of the OPR candle(s)** — if the OPR 5- or 15-minute candle is very wide (a long green or red candle with strong volume), it's usually better to wait for a pullback or, worst case, a mini consolidation before entering — this reduces initial risk and allows a tighter, better-defined stop.
6. **Assess remaining potential ("fuel in the tank")** — compare the current day's ATR (1-day average true range) to the past 1–2 years of ATR data to gauge how large a daily move this stock has historically been capable of, and compare that to the move already realized so far that day.
7. **Check for resistance/support blocks** — identify major resistance or support levels above/below current price, as covered previously.
8. **Decide position size and stop placement** — based on all the above, decide whether to take a full regular position size or a reduced one. Stop placement is generally below the low of the opening candle(s) or below the pre-market session low (whichever is more appropriate), somewhere within the 2×ATR range for the relevant timeframe.

**Final note on stocks "in play":** a stock with a gap, news, and institutional participation will typically stay active for at least the first three hours of the session, where most volume occurs. If you miss the initial open, you'll usually get a later chance to get involved — often via a B2B-style pullback entry — especially if your assessment shows the stock still has more potential in play.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Revolution Lighting Technology
Daily chart showed decent volume with a clean, one-month daily breakout level with no major EMA/resistance blocking the upside. On the 5-minute chart, the first candle was tight, but the market consolidated and pulled back toward the EMA 10/20 before curling back up. Rather than waiting for a strict OPR breakout, a **B2B-style entry** was used: enter once price curls back above the EMA 7 (a fast-reacting EMA commonly used on short timeframes like 5- or 10-minute charts — using EMA 7 vs. 10 makes little practical difference), stop tight below the mini-consolidation low, TP1 set at the high of the OPR. Clean daily setup + no resistance overhead = decent upside potential.

### Example 2: vTv Therapeutics
Similar setup: decent volume day, tight open on the 5-minute chart, but the pullback to the EMA 10/20 was shallower this time, so the standard **OPR 15-minute breakout** rule was used directly (it took roughly ten 5-minute candles to form the equivalent breakout). Entry on the OPR 15-minute breakout, tight stop below the low of the first candle, TP1 at a risk/reward ratio of 1, then stop moved to breakeven to let the rest of the day play out. The daily chart showed a very clean breakout with full EMA convergence (10 > 20 > 50 > 200, price above all), the classic "easy setup" with good potential toward the prior daily high around 780 (from a base near 700).

### Example 3: A High-Volume Mover (~38.7M Shares)
Huge relative volume day vs. recent average. Tight first 5-minute candle, followed by a pullback, then entry on the curl back above the OPR 5-minute high. Tight stop below the mini consolidation. Clean daily breakout above major resistance with full EMA convergence — no EMAs or resistance blocking the move. As the trade progressed, repeated B2B-style pullback/re-entry opportunities appeared (pullback to EMA 20, bounce off EMA 50), useful for pyramiding or repeated intraday in-and-out entries.

### Example 4: A Wide-Range Gapper (~17M Shares)
Huge volume day (vs. near-zero average recently), but the opening 5-minute range was quite wide. Waited for the breakout of the OPR 5-minute high (which coincided with the OPR 15-minute breakout, effectively three 5-minute candles combined). Stop placed under the mini consolidation formed by those three candles rather than far below. Clean daily breakout above two levels (252, then 430), full EMA convergence, no resistance overhead — a trade worth taking given the potential on display.

### Example 5: An Opening Gap With a Delayed 15-Minute Confirmation
Big volume day with an opening gap. The OPR 5-minute breakout triggered on the second 5-minute candle, but the OPR 15-minute breakout didn't confirm until after price consolidated and eventually broke out around 11:00. Here, one option is to take a **reduced size** on the initial 5-minute breakout, then add to the position on a pullback to the EMA 10 as price first curls up, using a tight stop on the second entry (or, since the difference wasn't large, simply keeping the stop below the low of the first 5-minute candle). The daily chart wasn't as clean as the previous examples — two resistance obstacles were visible, around 445 and 544 — which, combined with the gap, argued for reduced size on the initial breakout and waiting for the pullback re-entry (targeting TP1 at the high of the OPR 15-minute).

### Example 6: Another Gap-Up Breakout (~2M Shares Traded)
A large green 5-minute candle on strong relative volume, breaking clean above previously identified resistance levels (around 5465, 5537 in the transcript's price notation). Entry on the OPR 15-minute breakout, tight stop below the small consolidation, TP1 at a risk/reward ratio of 1, stop to breakeven afterward. A subsequent B2B-style pullback to the EMA 10/20 area, followed by a curl back above the EMA 10, offered a second entry with its own TP1 target at the prior high. The overall move ran from just above $5 to almost $9 — nearly 100% in a few hours.

### Example 7: A Stock Fighting Through Its EMA 50
Gap-up open, but the stock had to contend with the daily EMA 50 as initial resistance — a level that can be difficult to pierce, making aggressive buying at that level tricky even with a strong scanner alert. However, with relative volume around 20–30× average alongside a solid news catalyst, a large ATR day should be expected regardless of the EMA 50 obstacle. The next resistance level above was identified around 150. Checking the past year's average daily ATR (~$0.60–0.70, and about $0.63 at the time of the snapshot) confirmed the day had genuine potential for a big move even though price was starting only $0.05–0.10 away from the EMA 50 — comparing current-day conditions to historical ATR is the key technique for gauging expected range. Large green candles with strong volume early in the move are also often a sign of institutional accumulation — institutional players aiming for much more than a quick $0.05–0.10 scalp.

### Example 8: Zion Oil & Gas
Bought on the OPR 5-minute breakout just above 245 (price notation). Stop placed about $0.15 below; TP1 at 260 for a risk/reward ratio of 1. After TP1 filled, price pulled back all the way to the EMA 50, then the stop on the remaining position was hit at breakeven (245). Applying the B2B strategy again: re-entered long once price bounced off the EMA 50 and curled back above the EMA 7. Tight stop below the consolidation/EMA 50 area (~$0.10), TP1 on the second entry at 249, TP2 at 260, with the leftover position carried overnight. This was a "patience" trade — a market advancing in stages (advance, pullback, advance, pullback, then a final push through the two-day high) rather than one immediate massive move — a good example of why maintaining a watch list of recent movers for up to two weeks is worthwhile (covered in the next chapter of this module). Roughly a week after this initial trade, the same stock produced another massive move (breaking a one-month high on the 60-minute chart), running from around $2 to almost $5 — more than 100% in a single day — again entered via the OPR 5-minute breakout, with TP1/TP2 hit early and the remainder carried for several days before being stopped out on a trailing stop.

### Example 9: A Clean Donchian Breakout Setup
Very clean daily breakout above the Donchian channel levels, with full EMA convergence (10 > 20 > 50 > 100 > 200, price above all) — the same easy, high-probability setup described earlier, with no major obstacles overhead. On the 5-minute chart, the first candle didn't offer an immediate breakout; instead there was a consolidation around the EMA 7/20 before price curled back up above the EMA 7. Traders willing to be patient could enter on this curl (stop below the EMA 20 or below the first 5-minute candle's low) rather than waiting strictly for the OPR 5-minute breakout. TP1 quickly achieved, stop moved to breakeven around the 1275 level (price notation).

### Example 10: A Flat-Open Stock That Ran 44%
Opened essentially flat/slightly negative, but finished the day up 44%. The scanner flagged it about 8 minutes into the session, with the stock trading just under 240 (price notation). The daily chart showed clean EMA convergence with no immediate EMA 200 obstacle (far overhead) and the first resistance zone between 290 and 329. With about $0.50 of room to the first resistance and further potential up to 330, plus high relative volume and a solid news catalyst, the OPR 5-minute breakout (which had just occurred) was a reasonable entry offering a tight stop and strong risk/reward.

### Example 11: A 13% Gap-Up That Ran 44%
Opened with a 13% gap up — a modest gap by comparison, illustrating that even smaller opening gaps (flat, 5%, 10%) can still result in 50–100%+ days. The scanner flagged the stock right from the open, trading around 980 (price notation). The daily chart showed a clean 12-month-high breakout, with the OPR 5-minute high just broken, huge relative volume (~9–10 million shares vs. a much lower average), a significant news catalyst, and heavy social-media chatter among intraday traders (an additional scanner signal covered in the next module). Entry offered a tight stop and good risk/reward; for the rest of the session, price stayed cleanly above both VWAP and the EMA 20 — a genuine trend-following day, and a great example of a strong daily pattern/setup translating into a strong intraday trade.
