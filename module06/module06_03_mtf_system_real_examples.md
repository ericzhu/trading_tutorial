# Module 6, Video 3: Applying the MTF Conversion System — Real Trade Examples

In the last two videos we covered the three core strategies (MRA, Breaking Loose, B2B) and the Multiple Time Frame (MTF) conversion system that ties them together with risk, money, and position management. This video walks through real chart examples of applying the full MTF checklist — first on an FX pair (GBP/NZD), then on two stocks (AMD and Tandem Diabetes).

## Example 1: GBP/NZD (Swing Trading, FX)

For this swing/medium-term trade setup: **Screen #1 = Daily**, **Screen #2 = 240-minute (4-hour)**, with the weekly used for big-picture context and a 60-minute screen used later to fine-tune entries.

### Weekly Context
Historically, the market had consolidated for a long time under a major area of weekly resistance/support convergence. There was a first fake breakout — price ripped through, then came back down, found support on the 10 and 20 EMAs, then ripped again to a new high, before finally reintegrating back into the big consolidation/resistance area. Once the weekly candle closed back below the 10 and 20 EMAs and reintegrated the breakout/consolidation zone, this signaled trouble — confirmed by a weekly bearish MACD divergence (new price highs not reflected on the histogram or the MACD line).

### Screen #1 (Daily) — Strategic Decision
On the daily, price moved back below the 20 EMA and reintegrated the consolidation/breakout zone, with the 10/20 EMA crossing down and, a few days later, the 50 EMA reintegrating the breakout area too — a convergence of moving averages turning down. This is a textbook **Major Reversal Warning (MRA)** strategy setup. Based on this, the strategic decision was made: **play this market from the short side.**

### Screen #2 (240-Minute) — Counter-Trend Tactical Entry
With the strategic direction set to short, the next step is to find a counter-trend entry on the 240-minute chart — a point where the market rallies back up into resistance, offering a good point to rejoin the main (down) trend. This means looking for convergence of:
- Major resistance/support levels carried down from the weekly and daily (horizontal trend lines marking former support that should now act as resistance),
- The EMA 50 on the 240-minute chart (which regularly acts as major resistance/support in an established trend),
- The Stochastic RSI oscillator reaching overbought territory.

This is essentially applying the **B2B (back-to-trend)** strategy: the market curls down from overbought territory, off major resistance convergence, back below the EMA 10 — the confirmation signal to go short.

#### Trade Comparison — Two Possible Entries
**Trade 1 (first signal):** Short below the EMA 10, in overbought territory, right at the resistance box.
- Stop: ~150 pips (roughly 2× ATR — average ATR on the 240-minute chart was about 84 pips).
- TP1: risk/reward ratio of 1.
- Result: the trade was taken on November 1st, but TP1 wasn't reached until around November 7th — a valid trade, but it took longer than expected to play out, without ever hitting the stop.

**Trade 2 (better signal, a few days later):** Short again below the EMA 10, still in overbought territory per the Stochastic RSI, right back in the resistance box, with a pullback onto the EMA 50 on the 240-minute chart.
- Stop: ~104 pips.
- TP1: risk/reward ratio of about 1.88 — a noticeably better trade.
- Result: from the entry, the market corrected all the way from about 193.50 down to 190.65 — a 300-pip pullback — before resuming. Extending the target to the first consolidation area on the way down gave a risk/reward ratio of nearly 5 relative to the initial risk.

This illustrates that not every valid trade behaves as expected immediately — sometimes the market takes a few extra trading days to resume the trend, and sometimes (as with Trade 2) it delivers an excellent risk/reward outcome once it does.

### Fine-Tuning the Entry with Screen #3 (60-Minute)
Using the 60-minute chart as a third, shorter screen to fine-tune entries (rather than relying only on Screens #1 and #2) can produce a tighter stop and faster TP1.

- **First fine-tuned entry:** price retraced to the EMA 200 on the 60-minute chart (a major resistance level on that timeframe), inside the daily resistance box, indicators overbought. Entering short below the EMA 10 with a stop of about 90 pips achieved TP1 (risk/reward ratio of 1) very quickly. After TP1, the stop was moved to breakeven — the trade was later stopped out at breakeven, which is fine since TP1 profit was already banked.
- **Second fine-tuned entry:** another retracement into the daily resistance box, hitting the EMA 200 again (price had already retraced 300 pips, from 193.45 to 196.50), curling down below the EMA 10. Stop placed just above the recent high gave a risk/reward ratio of about 1.4 for TP1 — more than the minimum needed. After TP1, the market kept trending down without looking back, with the stop moved to breakeven and the rest of the position managed per standard position-management rules.
- Comparing stop sizes: on the 60-minute chart, average ATR was about 50 pips, so 2× ATR ≈ 100 pips — the fine-tuned entries (90–120 pips) were tighter than the original 240-minute-based entries (150/104 pips), allowing faster, more efficient TP1 execution.

**Takeaway:** Screen #1 (daily) sets the strategic bias (short, based on EMA convergence turning down). Screen #2 (240-minute) is used to find the counter-trend tactical entry back into the downtrend. An optional Screen #3 (60-minute) can further tighten the stop and speed up TP1, without changing the overall strategic or tactical logic.

## Example 2: AMD (Advanced Micro Devices) — Swing Trading, Stock

Screen #1 = Daily, Screen #2 = 60-minute (a 15-minute screen could be used to fine-tune, but this example only uses two screens).

### Historical Context
AMD ran up to $43 in 2006, then entered a long bear market, bottoming out and trading in a range between $1.50 and $10 for years. From early 2016, a massive rally took the stock to $15, where it met its first wall of sellers, then consolidated in a $10–$15 range from March 2017 to March 2018. From April 2018, the stock accelerated sharply — a fake breakout first, then a bounce back into the breakout zone, followed by a massive rally from roughly $15–16 to $35.

### Screen #1 (Daily) — Marking Key Levels
Key weekly/daily levels were marked from the prior consolidation range and past resistance zones (using Fibonacci retracement as well — the 38.2%–50% retracement zone lined up closely with a daily support area, also near the EMA 50). These levels were later used to plan TP1, TP2, TP3, TP4, etc.

### Screen #2 (60-Minute) — Counter-Trend Entries
Looking for counter-trend entries where price pulls back into a convergence of: the daily support/resistance box, the EMA 200 on the 60-minute chart, and Stochastic RSI oversold readings.

- **First pullback signal:** an engulfing pattern appeared, but since confirmation requires price to close back above the EMA 10, the trade wasn't taken yet. Once price closed above the EMA 10:
  - Stop: $0.63; TP1 target gave a risk/reward ratio of only 0.84, and price was also very close to the EMA 50 (which it failed to break through) — **this trade was skipped** since the ratio wasn't comfortable enough.
- **Second pullback signal:** oversold Stochastic RSI again, first candle closing above the EMA 10/20.
  - Stop placed below the recent 60-minute lows, TP1 giving a risk/reward ratio of **1.34** — an acceptable trade.
  - Stop of $0.47 compared reasonably to an average ATR of about $0.20 (2× ATR ≈ $0.40) — consistent with normal stop sizing.
  - After TP1, stop moved to breakeven, remaining position managed per the established TP2/TP3/etc. levels from the daily/weekly.

### A Clean Breakout Trade
Later, another oversold Stochastic RSI signal lined up with the EMA 200 on the 60-minute chart and prior daily support levels, as price pulled back from almost $17 to about $15.75. The market then gapped up above prior 60-minute Donchian resistance levels — a strong breakout confirmation with full EMA convergence (10 above 20, above 50, above 200).
- Entry: long on the breakout past the high of the prior candle, stop about $0.70 below.
- TP1 risk/reward ratio: more than 2 (average ATR pre-jump was about $0.20–0.25, so 2× ATR ≈ $0.40–0.50; the $0.70 stop was comfortably justified by the strength of the setup).
- This "easy setup" — full EMA convergence with price trading above all EMAs in ascending order (200 below 50 below 20 below 10 below price) — is the type of momentum trade to be aggressive on.

**Overall:** using daily as Screen #1 and 60-minute as Screen #2, several entries were available via B2B pullbacks and Breaking Loose breakouts, all validated by convergence of daily/60-minute support-resistance levels and Stochastic RSI oversold readings. Once TP1 is banked and the stop moved to breakeven, the trade becomes a "free option" against the remaining TP2/TP3/TP4 targets mapped from the daily/weekly chart (in this case, price came within a few cents of TP3 at 34.12, just short of the 34.55 target, before the highs).

## Example 3: Tandem Diabetes (TNDM) — Stock

### Weekly/Daily Context
TNDM was in a long bear trend from the end of 2013 (from around $300 down to near $1–3), then based and slowly started picking up around $2.50. On the daily, watch for unusual gap-up days with heavily elevated volume — a key scanner signal.
- Normal average volume: roughly 200,000–300,000 shares/day.
- On breakout days: over 5 million shares — roughly 20× average volume, later building to 800,000–900,000 average daily volume as the move developed.

This volume surge accompanied the stock moving above its 50-day moving average for the first time (an initial attempt that met a wall of sellers from investors trapped at higher prices from years before), briefly running from about $2.50 to $4 in days before retreating back to base. A second, more substantial volume surge then drove a clean breakout above the established resistance levels, with EMAs converging bullishly (10 above 20 above 50 above 100, with the 200 still overhead but far away). This is the point the stock entered the daily watchlist with a long strategic bias — price broke through $4.50 with the next major resistance (EMA 200) around $7, leaving roughly $3 of room to the upside.

### Screen #2 (60-Minute) — Breakout Entry
On the breakout candle (and the following one), with average ATR around $0.12:
- Stop: roughly $0.25–0.30 below the low of the prior candle.
- First half of position entered on the breakout; second half added after a small consolidation near the EMA 10 as price resumed upward.
- TP1 target for the second half gave a risk/reward ratio of about 1.

Both halves reached TP1 quickly, and the trade rode the resulting uptrend.

### Screen #1 (Daily) — Planning Further Targets
Additional horizontal resistance levels (including the EMA 200 on the daily) were marked to plan TP2, TP3, TP4, etc. as the position ran.

### Screen #2 (60-Minute) — Pullback Re-Entries
After banking early profits, the strategy shifts to looking for pullback re-entries on Screen #2 as the market continues its uptrend:
- **Pullback 1:** price fell from almost $8 to $6.60, Stochastic RSI oversold near the EMA 20 on the 60-minute chart. Entry once price curled back above the EMA 10:
  - Stop: about $0.36–0.38 (average ATR ≈ $0.20, so 2× ATR ≈ $0.40).
  - TP1: risk/reward ratio of about 1 (with the old highs offering a potential ratio of 2.3 if reached — but TP1 is deliberately taken early around a ratio of ~1).
  - Result: TP1 was hit, stop moved to breakeven — but the next day gapped down past the breakeven stop, executing roughly $0.20–0.25 below breakeven. Net result: still profitable or breakeven overall once combined with the TP1 gain — a reminder that not every trade is a full winner, but the overall approach still works out.
- **Pullback 2:** another oversold Stochastic RSI signal, price retraced from $9 to $8.46 (about a $0.70 correction) before curling back up. Entry above the EMA 10 with a stop of about $0.40–0.44 (vs. average ATR of $0.20–0.25, so 2× ATR ≈ $0.40–0.50) gave a risk/reward ratio of about 0.85 — an acceptable trade, especially given a strong close on above-average volume. The next day gapped up, executing TP1 at a much better ratio of about 2.

**Overall:** the same daily-as-Screen-#1/60-minute-as-Screen-#2 MTF approach applies here as with AMD — breakout entries and B2B pullback entries, both filtered through Stochastic RSI oversold/overbought readings and EMA convergence, with an optional 15-minute Screen #3 available to further tighten stops. Fine-tuning the entry only affects the entry itself, not the overall strategic (Screen #1) or tactical (Screen #2) decision-making process.
