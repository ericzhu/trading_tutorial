# Module 6, Video 2: The Breaking Loose Strategy, the B2B Strategy, and the Multiple Time Frame Conversion System

## The Breaking Loose Strategy

The Breaking Loose strategy is essentially a breakout/breakdown strategy, building on the breakout patterns covered in Module 2, Video 3 and its accompanying workbook on the basic tenets of technical analysis.

### Typical patterns
- **Rectangular channel breakout:** price consolidates between a lower and upper trend line, then breaks through the "wall of sellers/offers" — the target is usually the equivalent of the consolidation range. The same logic applies to a downside breakdown.
- **Flat-base consolidation breakout:** similar shape, flat base, then a sharp breakout.
- **Ascending triangle breakout:** a closely related pattern to the channel/flat-base setup, just shaped differently.

### Why support/resistance flips (the psychology behind it)
Quoting Alexander Elder (a psychiatrist before becoming a trader): *"When most traders and investors buy or sell short, they make an emotional and financial commitment to their trade. Their painful memories are the reason why the areas that served as strong support on the way down become strong resistance on the way back up, and vice versa."* Once you buy at a price and watch it fall, the moment it revisits your entry price you become emotionally driven to exit at breakeven — this collective behavior is what turns old support into new resistance and vice versa.

### Breaking Loose Rules

1. **Look for breakouts/breakdowns around the Donchian channels** of 5-day, 1-month, 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month lengths. Horizontal support/resistance lines (via Donchian channels) are strongly preferred over more subjective, hand-drawn trend lines. The exact breakout level doesn't need to match a Donchian line precisely — use good judgment to define a breakout price zone, since these channels can cluster closely together.
2. **Sizing:** typically play only 50% of the regular position size on the initial breakout.
3. **Adding to the position:** add the remaining size on either:
   - A pullback near the breakout area, or
   - A pullback to the EMA 10, 20, or 50 (depending on whether the move is parabolic or gradual), or
   - A breakout from a mini-consolidation (flag or triangle pattern) as a last resort if the move is very powerful.
4. **Mini-consolidation breakout entries** are based on taking out the previous candle's high/low or an engulfing pattern — not on Donchian channel levels (those are used for the initial setup identification/target zones, not for the mini-consolidation add-on entries).
5. **Best setup:** price above its moving averages, with the breakout occurring in the main direction of the trend/EMAs — this dramatically increases the probability of success. Only take breakouts that align with the main trend; never trade a breakout against the primary moving-average trend.
6. Because breakouts often turn out to be fake, stay prudent: reduce size and wait for confirmation before adding to the trade.

### Common Pitfalls
1. **A good breakout should work immediately.** If stopped out on a fake breakout, don't be afraid to re-enter later — professionals will try 2–3 times before succeeding or giving up.
2. **Holding too long after a failed breakout.** If price convincingly re-enters the breakout zone (and TP1 hasn't been secured with the stop at breakeven), exit at least 50% of the position rather than waiting.
3. **Entering too late after missing the move (FOMO).** It's better to wait for a proper entry with a decent stop and risk-reward ratio than to chase and end up with a poor entry, a distant stop, and an unfavorable RR.
4. **Lack of patience to wait for a pullback.** If you missed the initial move, wait for price to pull back toward the breakout area or curl back before re-entering — there will always be another trade.
5. **Not being prepared when the market is about to break out.** Use conditional/stop orders in advance (e.g., a buy-stop order for 50% of normal size) so you're not left behind. (Note: pre-empting the breakout before it happens is a different style — used by some scalpers — but not the approach favored here, which requires confirmed price action through the breakout level.)

## The B2B (Back to Trend) Strategy

B2B is essentially a **trend-following strategy** — "the trend is our friend." 

### How to implement it
1. Look for a pullback into the **EMA 10, 20, 50, 100, or 200** on the relevant timeframe (daily, 4-hour, 15-minute, etc., depending on the asset's type of momentum — parabolic, supernova, or gradual).
   - As a general rule, the **EMA 50** very often acts as strong support/resistance within the main trend regardless of timeframe — a good default if you want to avoid being overwhelmed by tracking all of 10/20/50/100/200.
   - The **EMA 200** very often serves as the last-resort level for establishing a trend — a break through it strongly signals a major trend change.
   - However, when EMA slopes are steep and the trend is well established, the EMA 10/20 also frequently act as strong support/resistance, especially early in the trend.
2. Once price nears a pullback area, look for signs of it **curling back** in the direction of the main trend — an engulfing pattern or a breakout of the recent candle's high/low.
3. **Confirmation rule:** require the candle to close above (uptrend) or below (downtrend) the **EMA 10** of the relevant timeframe before re-entering.
4. Same risk-reward rule as always: minimum RR of 1, with the stop loss set around 2× ATR of the relevant timeframe or at the recent high/low.

## The Multiple Time Frame Conversion (MTF) System

This section revisits and builds on multiple-timeframe analysis (Module 4, Video 2), combining all three core strategies (MRA, Breaking Loose, B2B) with the risk/money/position management rules into one complete system. This approach is adapted from Alexander Elder's "triple screen" strategy — in the spirit of Marty Schwartz's description of himself as "a synthesizer of other people's strategies and other people's strengths," rather than reinventing the wheel.

### Why multiple screens/timeframes are needed
Markets are made up of huge, varied crowds of traders and investors — technical analysis is essentially "applied mass social psychology." Because markets are this complex, no single timeframe is sufficient. Traders who focus on only one timeframe (e.g., only daily, or only 5-minute charts), or who constantly switch between timeframes inconsistently, end up with a confused, incoherent decision-making process and typically lose, especially if they focus only on short-term timeframes and lose sight of the bigger picture.

### Recap: Multi-timeframe analysis basics (from Module 4, Video 2)
- **Top-down analysis:** monthly → weekly → daily → hourly → 15-min → 5-min → 1-min.
- **Convergence:** the more timeframes that share the same trend direction, the higher the probability of success when trading in that direction.
- **Longer timeframes** (monthly, weekly, daily): determine the longer-term trend and the "big picture" — used to assess whether there's still potential/room left in the market and whether the estimated risk-reward justifies taking the trade.
- **Intermediate timeframes** (1-hour/60-min for stocks; 4-hour for FX or near-24-hour commodities like gold/crude): determine the medium-term trend.
- **Shorter timeframes** (15-min, 5-min, 1-min, or even tick charts): used mainly for trade setups and to fine-tune entries — not primarily for determining trend. Looking at a shorter timeframe can also reveal a mini-consolidation that offers a tighter stop and better risk-reward than what the daily/4-hour chart alone would suggest.

### MTF System Checklist

1. **Choose your favorite trading timeframe** based on your availability, need for action, and lifestyle — this becomes **Screen #2 (intermediate timeframe)**.
2. **Multiply Screen #2 by a factor of ~4–5** to get **Screen #1 (long-term timeframe)**.
3. **Apply trend-following analysis on Screen #1** to reach a strategic long/short decision, based on the ongoing main trend. Recap of trend-identification signs (from Module 4, Video 1):
   - EMAs clearly turning up/down, with price moving along/above/below them.
   - EMAs must be moving (parallel or separating) — never flat; steeper slopes are better.
   - Ideal alignment in an uptrend: EMA 10 > EMA 20 > EMA 50 > EMA 200 (reverse for downtrend).
   - Dow Theory confirmation: higher highs/higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs/lower lows (downtrend).
   - Donchian channels across multiple lengths (5-day, 1/3/6/12-month) forming clear support/resistance "steps" — the longer the Donchian timeframe, the stronger the level.
4. **Return to Screen #2** to search for a **tactical counter-trend entry point** in the direction of the main trend established on Screen #1 — "always trade in the direction of the tide, entering when the wave goes against the tide."
5. **Use the Stochastic RSI oscillator** (developed by Chande and Kroll, a derivative of the RSI) on Screen #2 to identify overbought (~80%+) or oversold (~20% or below) counter-trend conditions. Match this with a convergence of resistance/support levels (Fibonacci, Donchian, or multi-timeframe confluence) to judge whether the overbought/oversold reading represents a good re-entry opportunity in the direction of the main trend.
6. **Enter the trade** using the MRA or Breaking Loose core strategies once a counter-trend entry area is identified, selecting stop-loss and TP1 levels off Screen #2, based on the risk/money/position management models, to confirm the trade's risk-reward justifies taking it.
7. **Fine-tune the entry (optional)** using a third, shorter screen (e.g., 15-minute or lower) — strictly for refining the entry point, nothing else.

### Example logic
If Screen #1 (weekly) shows an uptrend and Screen #2 (daily) has pulled back to support/oversold levels, you could place a buy-stop above the previous day's high (on Screen #2), or buy near the EMA 20/50/100/200 (whichever has been holding price so far). The core idea: the trend from Screen #1 is expected to reassert itself on Screen #2.

Combining this multi-timeframe convergence of trend and support/resistance with strict risk/money management dramatically increases the probability of success and allows for stress-free trading.

### The "easiest," highest-probability setup
Look for full convergence across the weekly, daily, 4-hour (FX)/1-hour (stocks), and 15-minute (or lower) timeframes simultaneously:
- The current candle's close is above the previous candle's close on **every** one of these timeframes (in an uptrend; reverse for downtrend) — this can be built into scanner criteria.
- Price is above EMA 10 > EMA 20 > EMA 50 (and ideally EMA 200) on the daily and on the 4-hour/1-hour and 15-minute charts (reverse for downtrend).

This "full conversion" of trend and moving-average alignment across multiple timeframes stacks probability strongly in your favor.

### Worked examples of full convergence
- **NZD/CAD (short):** weekly downtrend (price below EMA 20/50) → daily EMA 10 < 20 < 50 (and later < 200) → 4-hour chart shows the same perfect alignment (10<20<50<200), with repeated bounces off the EMA 50/20 acting as resistance → 15-minute chart confirms the same full convergence below all EMAs.
- **Cronos (CRON) stock (long):** weekly trend clearly up with strong EMA 10/20/50 convergence and price above them → same full convergence on the 60-minute chart (with Donchian channels forming visible "steps") → 15-minute chart shows the same alignment, with the EMA 200 acting as an ultimate uptrend support level for a strong bounce. Stochastic RSI is also referenced as a supporting indicator here.

### Recommended Triple-Screen Combinations by Trading Style

| Trading style | Screen #1 (long-term) | Screen #2 (intermediate) | Screen #3 (entry fine-tuning) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stock day trader** | 1 hour | 15 minutes | 5 minutes |
| **FX day trader** | 4 hours | 1 hour | 15 minutes |
| **Swing / medium-term trader** | Daily | 4 hours (FX) / 1 hour (stocks) | 15 minutes |
| **Long-term active investor** | Weekly | Daily | 4 hours (FX) / 1 hour (stocks) |

### Important Warning: Stick to Your Chosen Timeframe Combination
Once you've chosen your triple-screen combination (based on whether you're a day trader, swing trader, or long-term investor), **stick to it** for both entries and exits. Do not get distracted by shorter-term noise (e.g., checking the 5-minute chart while running a swing trade based on daily/hourly screens) — your stop loss and take-profit levels were set from Screen #2 and should be monitored/exited using that same timeframe logic, not a smaller one. In fact, TP2 and beyond often reference even higher timeframes (weekly/monthly) for major resistance/support convergence to set longer-term profit targets. It's fine to experiment and adjust your chosen style over time (e.g., moving from day trading to swing trading if the pace doesn't suit you), but always anchor your decision-making to a consistent, deliberately chosen timeframe combination that matches your personality, availability, and need for action.
