## Risk of Ruin Tables

In these tables, we calculate the risk of ruin based on three inputs: our win ratio, our risk per trade (as a percentage of equity), and our average payoff ratio. For our purposes, we're assuming we already have a system that gives us an edge, providing a payoff ratio better than 1 to 1.

The red zones tell us the percentage chance that we draw our account down to a quasi-unacceptable level — what we call the "risk of ruin": the risk of ruining our account, taking it close to zero. Looking in detail, it's quite revealing how the probabilities in these tables can range from a 100% likely chance of ruin (all red boxes) down to 0% (all green boxes).

Look at the difference between two tables: one assumes someone betting 1% on every single trade, while the other assumes someone betting 10% on every single trade. There is far more green in the 1%-risk table than in the 10%-risk table. This means that betting only 1% per trade, we could be wrong as much as 70% of the time (right only 30% of the time) and still be able to make money — provided we have an average payoff ratio of 3 to 1 (on average, we make three times as much as we lose). Being wrong 70% of the time and right only 30% of the time, we can still maintain our account without any real risk of ruining it down to zero.

With a little planning and thought, you can dramatically increase your odds of survival just by having respect for this risk-of-ruin formula. You don't need to be a mathematical genius or even know how to do the calculations yourself — just understand the principle and the impact your choice of risk-per-trade can have on your performance.

Keeping that in mind — and keeping in mind that the past does not necessarily predict the future — it is imperative that you continuously monitor your current win ratio and payoff ratio over time, in order to adjust, if needed, the percentage of risk you take on every single trade.

## What Is Money Management, Really?

Now let's turn to money management — what is it, really? I think a lot of people skip this part completely — they're just eager to start trading, clicking that buy/sell button, and they basically forget everything once they're in front of their screen. But as we've started to understand, this is a huge mistake. Some people also think of money management as only being about the trade exit or position management.

Let me explain how I personally see money management — the three main principles/components I include in the overall concept:

### 1. Position Sizing

The first crucial component, which we've already touched on: finding the size that's adapted to you and to your account. "Adapted" means adapted to your trading style, to the size of your account, and to your psychology.

### 2. Diversification

The second core component is diversification — but hear me out, because diversification can be very overrated. My concept of true risk diversification relies on true portfolio risk and equalization of volatility within that risk. This is essentially the risk parity portfolio concept of Ray Dalio, of Bridgewater Associates — some of you may have heard of him; he runs one of the biggest hedge funds in the world, I believe around $150 billion under management in one fund alone (he manages even more across his other funds), and he's a very, very smart guy. In my view, his risk parity portfolio concept is the only truly diversified portfolio management technique for the long-term investor — though this is really a subject for another time, another conversation, and perhaps an entirely separate course focused purely on money management and portfolio money management techniques.

That said, when I run my own portfolios, basic, simple diversification at the portfolio level consists of mixing the four main asset-class categories: equities (stocks), bonds, commodities, and cash. Why include cash as an asset class? Because when everything goes crazy — when things really hit the fan — your best bet is often to stay in cash, to stay liquid. You'll find that to be true many, many times.

Beyond these four main categories, I also usually include inflation-reactive real assets, such as real estate — this could be your own home, or a house/apartment you hold as a rental investment, so it's part of your overall asset portfolio; or, within a liquid portfolio of equities/ETFs, it could be a REIT (real estate investment trust) — there are many out there targeting different markets, countries, and types of real estate sectors. It could also be shipping — I know that sector quite well, having been involved in that business in the past. Ships are real assets that tend to depreciate over time, but can also act as a hedge against inflation, since they're automatically linked to commodities and transportation.

As you may remember from module four, even within each of these asset classes there are many sub-components — specific sectors or industries, bond maturities, bond types (corporate, government, high-yield, etc.). So even within these main categories (three, excluding cash), you can dig deeper into focused sectors, industries, and bond types to build diversification — either very focused or broad. That said, this diversification process, for me, needs to stay either at a basic level or be taken all the way to a mature, sophisticated level like the risk parity portfolio I mentioned — but we won't dwell on that here.

### 3. Position Management

The third component of money management, to me, is position management — this refers to exit strategies, specifically in good-case scenarios, where the trade we're already in is already profitable, and we're planning how to manage our paper profits: techniques like partial profit-taking, time exits, trailing stops, moving stops to breakeven, etc. We'll look at these position-management techniques a little later in this module.

These three pillars — position sizing, diversification, and position management — constitute what I define as money management, overall.

## Position Sizing in Practice

Back to a very important part of money management: position sizing. A usual mistake many traders make, especially beginners, is using the same size for every trade — e.g., always trading 100 or 200 shares of stock, or always trading exactly one unit when trading FX through a CFD or spread-betting broker, regardless of the trade.

Selecting the correct trade size is critical to minimizing your risk, because every trade is different — so every trade size should be different too. Remember, our main goal is preservation of capital. We've worked hard enough to build up the savings that constitute our trading capital, and we want to preserve it — because, as a reminder, it can be extremely difficult to recover from a massive loss, as we saw in our previous example.

**The math of drawdown recovery** shows just how brutal this can be:

| Drawdown | Gain needed to break even |
|---|---|
| 10% | ~11% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 30% | ~43% |
| 50% | 100% |
| 60% | 150% |
| 90% | 900% |

Once you've suffered a 50% drawdown in your account, you need a 100% gain just to recover. At 60%, you need 150%. At 90%, you need a 900% gain to recover. These become almost impossible targets — even given plenty of time, recovering that money becomes practically impossible.

By discussing the concept of risk of ruin, we've seen that the triple combination of trade size, win ratio, and average payoff ratio can have either a wonderful or a catastrophic effect on overall performance and trading accounts. If we make the wrong decisions from the start on the combination of these three parameters, we can dig ourselves into a hole from which it becomes simply impossible to climb out. Drawdowns of 50% or more in an account are very, very difficult to recover from.
