## The 14 Stages of the Investor's Emotional Cycle

In the first part of the video, we'll review the 14 stages of the investor's cycle of emotions and psychology. This is covered in the PDF as well, but it's worth reviewing again and internalizing, because it's so important to know where you are in this environment and at what stage of the investment cycle you might find yourself. If you start understanding your own feelings and psychology, and where you are in this cycle, you will respond to it differently. That's hopefully what this exercise will do for you.

Business schools typically teach the efficient market theory, which claims that the market is random and can't be beaten. These theories are based on the assumption that we are all rational people who enter a transaction intending to maximize gain and minimize loss. While that sounds great, the fact is that most investors are not the purely rational robots that efficient markets rely upon. Instead, our emotions often cloud our decision-making and prevent us from acting rationally. When things are great, we feel nothing can stop us; when things go bad, we look to take drastic action. Because emotions can be such a threat to our financial health, it's important to be aware of them — this awareness can protect us from the negative impact of impulsive, irrational reactions.

The next slide describes the common market psychology cycle nicely — how emotions evolve and the effect they have on our decisions. If we can better understand the stages of this cycle, we can tame our emotional rollercoaster. The cycle is made up of 14 stages.

**Stage one — Optimism.** This is usually how we all start. Investors expect things to go their way, expecting a return for the risk taken. It's common to get excited about the possibility of even greater returns, and this excitement becomes thrilling as returns exceed expectations.

**At the top of the cycle — Euphoria.** This is when investors experience literal euphoria, but it's also the point of maximum financial risk. We believe everything we touch turns to gold; we fool ourselves into believing we can beat the market and cannot make mistakes; excessive returns feel like the norm. We start tolerating higher levels of risk and putting on bigger trades. This is when it becomes really dangerous.

**The turn — Anxiety and denial.** The second phase occurs when the market stops meeting our great expectations and begins to turn. At first we anxiously watch the market for any sign of direction, and soon anxiety turns to denial. This is when we make our biggest mistake: transforming ourselves into so-called "long-term investors," i.e., finding any reason to rationalize our loss. Then, as the value of the investment declines quickly, we become fearful, not knowing how to act, desperately grasping at any idea that will get us back to breakeven. Many people then start acting defensively, perhaps switching out of risky assets into more defensive asset classes such as Treasury bonds, Treasury bills, or cash.

**Bear market reality — Panic and capitulation.** In the third phase, the realities of a bear market become clear, and many panic and withdraw from the market altogether. They capitulate, afraid of further losses, thinking "perhaps not for me." Those who keep going become despondent and discouraged, wondering whether markets will ever recover. Ironically, at this point of desperation, investors commonly fail to recognize that this timing actually corresponds to the point of maximum financial opportunity.

**Stage four — Skepticism and depression.** Here investors experience some skepticism, and some go through a phase of depression — thinking "how could I be so stupid, how could I be so wrong, how bad am I?" But this phase is what separates the big boys from the rest of the crowd. They start looking back at the trade and analyzing what went wrong. True traders are born here, learning from past mistakes and how to avoid them going forward. Eventually, investors come to realize that markets move in steps, and begin looking for the next opportunity, hoping next time will be all right — and the cycle starts all over again. The ones who make it through next time are the ones who internalize the lessons of their experience. It's okay to make mistakes; it may even be the best thing that can happen to us at the beginning.

## Two Quotes Worth Remembering

Take a moment to consider two quotes — one from the famous multi-billionaire market wizard Bruce Kovner, and one from Bernard Shaw. When Bruce Kovner started out working at Commodities Corp., he had a mentor, Michael Marcus, who taught him certain things were crucial. Michael told him: "You have to be willing to make mistakes regularly, there's nothing wrong with it." He taught Kovner about making the best judgment and being wrong, making a second-best judgment and being wrong again, and making a third-best judgment — and then doubling the money.

The second quote, from Bernard Shaw, says success does not consist in never making mistakes, but in never making the same one a second time. Mistakes are part of our trading and investing process, and we have to accept them.

## The Consequences of the Emotional Rollercoaster

Emotion turns rational investors into irrational investors. So how do we avoid the temptation to fall into one of these traps? By developing and committing to a well-defined trading or investing strategy — a plan is the only way to protect ourselves from our emotions. Otherwise, our well-developed, so-called diversified long-term financial plans are placed in jeopardy when we're confronted by extraordinary events and simply become guided by emotion. This is where the role of a third party — a financial advisor, trading mentor, or coach — becomes of utmost importance: they can help separate your emotions from reality and steer you onto the path of rational investing.

## Facing the Facts About Risk

Trading is inherently risky, and to my knowledge there is no trade with a guaranteed outcome — the possibility of being wrong and losing money is always present. So when you put on a trade, can you consider yourself a risk taker? This may sound like a trick question, but it isn't — the logical answer is yes. In fact, virtually all traders make this assumption, and most take pride in thinking of themselves as risk takers. The problem is that this assumption could not be further from the truth. Of course any trader is taking a risk when putting on a trade, but that doesn't mean he's entirely accepting that risk psychologically.

The best traders can put on a trade without the slightest hesitation or conflict, and just as freely admit when it's not working, getting out even with a loss without the slightest emotional discomfort. In other words, the risk inherent in trading doesn't cause the best traders to lose their discipline, focus, or sense of confidence. Simply put, the best traders are not afraid. They're not afraid because they've developed attitudes that give them the greatest degree of mental flexibility to flow in and out of trades based on what the market is telling them, while at the same time developing attitudes that prevent them from getting reckless.

If you're afraid of being wrong or losing money, you'll never learn enough to compensate for the negative effects these fears have on your ability to be objective and to act without hesitation. In other words, you won't be confident in the face of constant uncertainty. The hardcore reality of trading is that every trade has an uncertain outcome, and unless you learn to completely accept the possibility of this uncertainty, you'll try — consciously or unconsciously — to avoid any possibility you define as painful.

So what's the solution? You need to learn how to adjust your attitudes and beliefs about trading so that you can trade without the slightest bit of fear, while at the same time keeping a framework in place that doesn't allow you to become reckless. Nothing about trading is more central to your success — and more misunderstood — than the concept of taking responsibility and truly accepting risk, which means accepting the consequences of your trade without emotional discomfort or fear. It does you no good to take the risk of putting on a trade if you're afraid of the consequences. You need to create a new thinking strategy, a new relationship with the market — one that dissociates your trading from what it typically means to be wrong or to lose, and that prevents you from perceiving anything about the market as threatening. When the threat of pain is gone, the fear will correspondingly disappear, leaving you with a mind free to see what's available and to act on what you see. In fact, we're often amazed at how simple the solution to our problems really is — all we need is a subtle adjustment in our thought process.

## The Root Cause: No Trading Plan

Based on group studies Richard Krauss conducted, 90% of part-time traders picked the following as their biggest weaknesses in trading: first, lack of confidence; second, no trading plan; third, execution; and fourth, fear of loss. What single element is the root of the other three? What causes lack of confidence? What causes fear of loss? What causes poor execution (meaning fear of pulling the trigger)? The simple answer is: no trading plan, no strategy. This is the basic feature that separates losing traders from winning traders — lack of confidence, fear of loss, and hesitation on pulling the trigger all disappear in direct relation to the validity of a solid trading plan.

## Three Types of Traders

Now let's discuss three different types of traders — traders I've met throughout my life. You might be one of them, or you might become one of them. What matters is that you take the path to become the successful one, but to do that you need to understand the other paths, and the bad things that can happen to certain traders.

**The fun gambler.** The first type is the guy looking for excitement — he loves the adrenaline and rush of trading. He understands it takes a lot of work and time to get good at it, but he's not interested in working hard; he's looking for fun. Nevertheless, he still manages his losses, because he somehow internalizes the risk involved, and he speculates with small amounts of limited capital. Most of the time, I've seen this guy manage to keep it under control.

Funny enough, I like to quote Ed Seykota (Ezcurra), one of the famous traders quoted in the Market Wizards book: "Win or lose, everybody gets what they want out of the market. Some people seem to like to lose, so they win by losing money." I could not express this better, and I've seen it happen — highly successful entrepreneurs and business people, very smart people, somehow finding happiness in gambling, coming back into the markets and burning account after account after account. I'm sure they weren't looking to lose all this money — that's not what they did in their own business, where they had very successful, multi-million-dollar operations. But they found their emotional or psychological happiness in trading, opening and regularly funding accounts by losing. This sentence really puts it in true words: no matter what, everyone finds his own happiness in trading. So you have to be careful about that, and be able to catch yourself and understand where you are — whether you're that type of trader, or becoming one.

**The impulsive trader.** The second type is a bit more problematic. He's already quite experienced and has worked hard, and it definitely helps — he may be able to live from trading, or at least make some nice money, or, if retired, make some nice extra retirement income. He manages to win regularly, building his account equity quite nicely. However, because of his impulsivity, every now and then he'll burn an account, turning all his winnings into losses. Bad habits include playing small on winning positions and doubling down, or over-leveraging losing trades. Fundamentally he's not a bad trader at all — he's got the right components, the right fiber to become a good trader, and might even be very good at teaching other people. But he finds it very difficult to apply the same principles to himself. So really, he won't stop until he crashes straight into the wall, because he firmly believes he can make it in the markets.

This trader is left with few options: either he becomes an analyst (I'm joking, but it's not that far from reality), or he finally becomes conscious of his emotional bias. He starts avoiding impulsive trading, avoiding leverage, and sticking to his methodology and trading plan like he should. If he does all that, he'll eventually turn into the third type of trader — the one we're all aspiring to be.

**The pro trader.** This is the successful trader. This type already has a few years of trading behind him. He understands he needs to continuously work hard, and if he wants to be on top, he understands it's not about excitement or fun. On the contrary, he treats his trading or investing activity like a real business. He can be his own man, an independent trader like myself or yourself, or he could work for a boss — at a company, a bank, a fund, etc. — but one thing is for sure: this guy is not in it for the excitement. He's in it to make money. As my good friend Larry would put it, "I don't trade for excitement, I trade to win."

The pro trader has a well-tested methodology, and by well-tested I mean he's worked on it and fine-tuned it over the years. But like most of these guys, he hasn't invented anything — you don't need to invent anything, you don't need to be the creator of the Bollinger Band or the MACD indicator or whatever it is. Some of the people who invented these tools weren't even good traders. The one thing for sure is that you need to take pieces of the puzzle — use other people's ideas and principles as part of a bigger formula that works for you, that matches your personality, your attitude, the person you are.

I like to quote the market wizard Marty Schwartz, also known as the Pit Bull trader. He used to say about himself, "I'm a synthesizer of other people's ideas." He didn't take credit for coming up with his own methodology — he said, "I've used this guy, I've used this guy, I've used this guy, and I was working with my mentor, and that's how I came up with something that I liked and that fitted my personality." Marty Schwartz has always been very honest about this, and one book I can recommend to you is *Pit Bull*, in which he writes about his experience of how he started in the business and how he became so successful.

Back to our pro trader: once he's found his methodology, he follows it to the letter without any stress whatsoever. He's learned about taking a loss and jumping to the next opportunity without questioning. He's learned about trading with little or no leverage, because leverage is the factor that will tip him off his psychological balance — using it too aggressively is never good. He's learned to size his positions small enough to match his psychologically acceptable loss level, and with that, he's able to detach himself from the money factor — he doesn't bring his ego into the trade at all. He can sit in front of the screen doing nothing, waiting for the right opportunity, waiting for the signals his methodology gives him. That means trading one to three times a day at most if he's into intraday trading, or maybe only one to three times a week if he's into swing trading, or he might not trade at all — because that too is part of trading. He understands his trading success isn't built on the big home run or swinging for the fences, but rather on a multitude of trades and consistency. His game plan is really about making regular small gains as opposed to shooting for the moon — we like to call this approach "hitting singles."

The pro trader is also a great portfolio manager. He understands and manages his overall risk, and he's certainly not the type holding hundreds of positions at the same time. Even in a complete, well-diversified portfolio, I would never suggest holding that many positions to anyone — not even in the portfolios I manage for other people. At the end of the day, diversification can be highly overrated; you need a well-diversified portfolio of unlinked and uncorrelated assets, but not in the sense of pure correlation — we'll talk about that in another module. Back to this pro trader: he's passionate about trading and investing, and all of his hard work doesn't feel like hard work to him at all.

## The Magic Formula: Three Points

So how do you become this third type of guy, this successful pro trader? Here's the magic formula, in three main points.

1. **Develop your own methodology and trading plan, and stick to it.** This strategy of yours must have an edge — meaning at least a 51% probability of winning trades versus losing trades, with a minimum risk-to-reward ratio of 1 to 1. We'll provide you with such a methodology, in all its details, in Module 6. From there, you'll be able to build around it to match your own style and personality.

2. **Stick to strict risk and money management policies** that fit closely with your psychology and personality. You need to be able to detach yourself from the monetary value of the trade and treat every single trade independently.

3. **Concentrate exclusively on the process** and follow it for a minimum of six months without questioning it. Without a strict methodology, you'll be like being in the middle of the sea without a compass. Try concentrating on just one or two financial instruments to start with, in order to understand and apply your strategy every single day, to the letter.

If you can start by implementing these three main points religiously, I can promise you'll already be many steps ahead of the majority of so-called traders, and you'll be able to achieve miracles.
