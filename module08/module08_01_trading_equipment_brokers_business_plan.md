# Module 8, Video 1: Getting Organized — Equipment, Charting Software, Brokers, and Your Trading Business Plan

The previous modules covered the essential material of the trading method itself. Module 8 shifts focus to getting properly organized: what equipment, software, and broker setup you actually need to get started, and — just as importantly — how to build a realistic trading business plan around your own goals, risk tolerance, and personality.

This video walks through:

- The hardware you need (and don't need) to get started.
- Charting software options.
- Broker options, and the Pattern Day Trader (PDT) rule.
- Building a trading business plan — objectives, achievable goals, and costs.

## Desktop vs. Laptop: One Possible Setup

The instructor's own office setup uses a six-monitor desktop PC, with a laptop connected alongside it:
- The laptop is networked with the desktop so files can be synced and backed up at the start and end of each day. This is especially useful for anyone who isn't a fan of cloud backup storage — the software used for this is **SyncBack Pro**.
- The same keyboard and mouse are shared across the desktop and laptop using **Synergy**, so there's no need for a separate wireless keyboard or typing directly on the laptop — the mouse simply moves from the desktop screens onto the laptop screen.
- The laptop's main purpose in this setup is to run Outlook email, but it also doubles as an emergency device: if the power or main internet connection goes down, the laptop can run on its own battery (up to 3–4 hours) and switch to a 4G hotspot very quickly.

### Power Protection: Use a UPS

A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) is highly recommended to protect against sudden blackouts. It gives you enough time to close positions, save your work, and shut down the computer properly, and it also protects your hardware against sharp power fluctuations.

### Internet Connection

Get the best internet connection speed you can afford — faster is better, though there's no need to go overboard. For the desktop PC specifically, prefer a wired **Ethernet** connection over Wi-Fi, since Ethernet is faster.

## Desktop PC Specs

These are the specs used personally, offered as a guide rather than a strict requirement:

1. **CPU (processor):** Intel Core i7, 2.8 GHz. The processor determines how many tasks the computer can execute per second — modern CPUs have multiple cores, and more cores generally means faster, more efficient performance.
2. **Hard drive:** A regular WD Black HDD, 500 GB. Storage is cheap these days, so upgrading isn't expensive. 500 GB is generally more than enough unless you plan to store large amounts of video/movies. An SSD (solid state drive) is faster and more efficient — used on the laptop in this setup.
3. **RAM:** 24 GB on the desktop. RAM determines how many programs can run simultaneously, which matters a lot for trading since you'll typically run the broker platform, charting software, and a spreadsheet all at once. A range of 8–16 GB should be enough for most people.
4. **Graphics card (GPU):** Essential for anyone running multiple displays. The GPU handles all video/graphics processing on screen — look for at least 2 GB of dedicated GPU memory, or 4 GB+ if using more than two monitors (think of GPU memory as RAM, but for graphics). Nvidia GeForce cards are used here; USB video adapters from StarTech are also used to drive a couple of the extra screens.
5. **Monitors:** A mix of BenQ and AOC monitors, ranging from 19 to 24 inches. You don't need to start that big — a good starting point is two monitors on the desktop (or one slave screen attached to a laptop being used as the main trading machine). Two screens give you enough space to monitor multiple windows at once without constantly switching. An alternative is a single ultra-widescreen "gamer" monitor, which is quite good but more expensive. Depending on the setup, you may also need a monitor stand (capable of holding multiple monitors) or a simpler desk mount.
6. **Cooling:** Adding hardware puts extra strain on the stock cooling system, and the CPU is usually the first component to suffer from heat overload. A single or dual-fan cooling system is a worthwhile investment; a liquid cooling unit is a fancier (and pricier) option.

## Laptop Specs and Use Case

Some traders are perfectly happy trading exclusively from a laptop as their main and only station — usually a high-performance gaming laptop, with slave monitors added if desired. The specs used personally are shared as a rough guide to be copied or improved on, not followed as a definitive plan.

### Desktop vs. Laptop: A Personal Choice

Ultimately, whether to trade from a desktop or laptop is a personal choice. The multi-monitor office desktop setup is preferred for the screen real estate it provides, letting every application sit in view at once — but this isn't required, especially when starting out.

If trading mainly from a laptop, it's still worth connecting at least one additional monitor to save time. Options include:
- A regular monitor at a fixed desk setup.
- A portable, USB-powered monitor for traders who are frequently on the move — shown here as part of a "holiday setup" used while staying at a summer house, alongside a couple of regular monitors also brought along. Portable monitors adapt to portrait or landscape orientation and connect easily via USB. Recommended brands: **AOC** and **Asus** (ZenScreen).

Even on the road, the goal is to maintain a minimum of two screens alongside the laptop.

## Charting Software

Charting platforms range from very poor to excellent, and some — like Bloomberg or KCG-tier platforms — are excellent but extremely expensive. Those costs made sense when working for a firm (which negotiates bulk discounts), but not as an independent retail trader footing the bill personally.

For retail traders, three platforms stand out as the best value for money (a personal choice, not an absolute ranking):

| Platform | Coverage | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **TC2000** | US stocks only, plus some FX (not its strongest feature) | Slightly less user-friendly; also offers broker connectivity, though the affiliated broker is not fully endorsed here |
| **TradingView** | Full global market coverage | Higher monthly fees due to broad coverage; generally more user-friendly |
| **ProRealTime (PRT)** | Full global market coverage | Higher monthly fees; strong interconnectivity with several good brokers for direct execution from the charts |

All three offer a free trial period, so it's worth testing each one and deciding which fits best. Also keep in mind that many broker platforms offer their own charting packages (quality varies widely), and some brokers offer PRT or TradingView at a pre-negotiated (or even waived, above a certain trading volume) fee. It's worth doing the math on which combination makes sense both from a usability and a cost standpoint.

## Brokers

Charting platforms handle analysis, but a broker is needed to execute trades. Note that account availability depends on where you live — some brokers only accept US-based customers, some exclude US customers, and some exclude residents of certain countries, with terms changing frequently, so checking eligibility is a necessary first step.

No single broker is "the best" — as with charting software, each has its own strengths, weaknesses, and fee structure (some charge monthly/annual platform fees or custodian fees, some have minimum account balances, some don't). The list below is a personal starting-point shortlist, not an exhaustive comparison:

- **FXCM** and **IG Index** — recommended for CFDs. Both offer solid platforms covering a large basket of instruments including single stocks (though these have not been personally traded through them). FXCM's **micro lots** (micro-sized contracts relative to the equivalent futures contract) make it a great choice for a beginner trader starting small in FX, commodities, indices, and bond futures.
- **Interactive Brokers (IB)** — the personal broker of choice. Offers some of the most competitive execution fees, and covers the full spectrum of international markets and instruments (CFDs, futures, stocks, ETFs, FX, mutual funds, etc.). It's a multi-billion-dollar, publicly listed company serving retail traders, hedge funds, prop desks, and financial advisors alike — a plus from a capital-security standpoint. The main downside: the platform is not the most user-friendly and takes some time to get used to, but the advantages outweigh that learning curve.
- **TD Ameritrade** — a major, long-established (~50 years old) US broker offering a very good product.
- **E-Trade** — also a solid, reliable platform.
- **Lightspeed** — not used personally, but reportedly well-regarded among active day traders, particularly those who rely heavily on hotkeys. IB also offers hotkeys, though they're considered less flexible from a programming perspective than Lightspeed's.
- **CenterPoint Securities** — reportedly a preferred platform for short sellers, with a stock locate/borrow facility said to be a cut above the competition (though this is not from personal experience, and these things change over time).

### The Pattern Day Trader (PDT) Rule

The **PDT rule** (Pattern Day Trader rule) is defined by **FINRA**, the US markets' central regulatory body. A pattern day trader is one who buys and sells the same security on the same day in a **margin account** (margin being borrowed money). If a trader executes four or more day trades within five business days, they're classified as a pattern day trader — unless those day trades represent 6% or less of all trades made over that five-day period.

Under the PDT rule, any margin account classified as a pattern day trader must maintain a minimum of **$25,000** in account equity to day trade without restriction. The rule only kicks in once the account's net liquidation value drops below that $25,000 threshold; if it does, and the trader exceeds the allowed number of day trades, the broker can — and will — freeze the account from trading, for up to 90 days.

In short: either maintain at least $25,000 to day trade actively without restriction, or accept limits on trading activity.

#### Ways Around the PDT Rule

**1. Own multiple brokerage accounts.** By spreading day trades across several accounts, it's possible to plan around the PDT threshold in each one individually. This has additional benefits beyond avoiding the PDT rule:
- More accounts mean access to more short borrows, useful for short-selling strategies.
- It diversifies counterparty/credit risk across multiple deposit institutions — the same philosophy applied to spreading funds across multiple banks, rather than keeping "all eggs in one basket."

The downside: more accounts means more complexity to manage, minimum balance requirements to maintain in each, and potentially more annual fees. On balance, though, the upside is considered worth it.

**2. Trade options instead of stocks (in a cash account).** According to FINRA, the PDT rule applies to day-trading options in a margin account, but *not* in a **cash account** (recall the original rule concerns margin accounts specifically). Trading options in a cash account avoids the PDT rule entirely. Additional advantages of options:
- Options settle in **one day**, versus **two days** for most stocks — cash becomes available to redeploy roughly twice as fast.
- Options require significantly less capital. For example, buying 100 shares of Facebook might require around $19,000–$90,000 in a cash account (figures as of the original recording), depending on leverage available via a margin account. By contrast, buying one at-the-money call option (representing 100 shares) might cost only $150–$500, depending on time to expiry — a dramatic difference in capital required for similar underlying exposure.
- As mentioned in Module 7, shorting via **put options** can be easier and safer than shorting stock outright — though not all stocks have listed options, and liquidity varies significantly from one name to another.

Options trading is somewhat more complex and will be covered in full in the final module (Module 9).

## Building Your Trading Business Plan

Before developing any trading system, it's essential to decide exactly what you want to accomplish. As Dr. Van Tharp puts it: *"Until you know where you want to go, you can never get there."*

### Step 1: Define Your Objective

Before starting, ask honestly: **what's my objective?** Is trading just a fancy pursuit, or a source of excitement away from a boring routine? These questions might feel generic or uncomfortable, but they force real self-reflection about your actual goals — and your objectives are a critical part of your system. As Bernard Baruch is quoted as saying: *"Win or lose, everybody gets what they want out of the market. Some people seem to like to lose, so they win by losing money."* This isn't a prediction of failure — it's a prompt to genuinely examine your real motivations.

Common objectives include:
- Extra income on top of a salary or pension.
- Long-term investing toward a comfortable retirement.
- Extra money for occasional pleasures (a summer holiday, a piece of jewelry).
- Going full-time and making trading a primary living.

### Step 2: Match Goals to Reality

Be realistic about what's achievable. For example, someone with $100,000 in living expenses who wants to cover the entire amount from trading a $100,000 account is effectively demanding a 100% return every single year — while also having to weather the very real possibility of a 20–40% (or worse) drawdown at any time, for any unknown duration. It's essential to weigh objectives and goals against this kind of realistic downside before assuming a plan is achievable rather than pure fantasy.

Realistic benchmarks:
- Top professional traders rarely exceed **20–40% annual return** on larger capital bases.
- Skilled retail "pro" traders (those who've gone through serious education) may achieve **50–100% a year**, but usually on much smaller accounts — it's considerably easier to compound 50–100% on $5,000–$10,000 than on $1,000,000+.
- Claims of turning $20,000 into millions via 1,000%+ returns should be treated with skepticism: such figures rarely come with real audited statements, and even when they do, the full picture (including other accounts) is often hidden. This doesn't mean it's impossible — only that it's far from easy, and only a handful of people sustain such returns consistently.

Achieving strong returns typically requires tolerating meaningful risk — usually drawdowns in the region of at least half the annual return being targeted. Every trader needs to know their own "uncle point": the number that becomes uncomfortable enough to signal "enough is enough." Once that number is known, risk management can be built around it, tailored to your own personality.

### Step 3: Assess Your Devotion (Available Time)

Be realistic about how much time can actually be dedicated to trading, since this directly shapes what kind of trading is feasible — echoing the earlier discussion (Module 4) on multiple time frame analysis and finding your favorite trading time frame. For example, someone with a full-time job who can only check the market in the evenings shouldn't build an initial plan around day trading — swing trading on longer time frames is the more realistic starting point. A later transition into day trading (and potentially a full-time career switch) is a separate, later-stage decision.

### Step 4: Know Your Personal Strengths and Weaknesses

Personal traits inevitably shape trading psychology. Some traders are nervous, compulsive, or afraid of losses; others are serial risk-takers who like to "hear the cash register ring" often. Some are patient and comfortable holding through big swings; others are highly disciplined, while some are not disciplined at all. Some are self-confident; others are stressed and lack conviction.

The better these strengths and weaknesses are defined, the faster you'll recognize which trading strategy, risk approach, and position management style truly fit — an impatient or insecure trader needs a very different approach than a patient, methodical, confident one.

### Putting It Together

Take time to genuinely answer these questions, write the answers down, and review them with an open mind — staying connected to the emotions that surface, since they're a big clue to any underlying psychological issues around investing or money in general. There's no right or wrong answer here, only *your* answer. Completing this self-assessment honestly builds a strong foundation for the trading business plan and represents genuine maturity before starting the journey into investing and trading.

## Estimating Startup Costs

Price out only what's needed, starting minimal and adding on over time:

1. **Hardware:** PC, laptop, or both.
2. **Display:** Start with two monitors if affordable — one is workable (laptop screen or a single monitor on a PC), but given how cheap monitors are today, the savings rarely outweigh the benefit of a second screen.
3. **Charting software:** One subscription, chosen based on the markets to be covered, plus whatever data package is needed. Some brokers offer their own charts or discounted access to major charting software — try the shortlisted platforms via their demo/free trial periods before committing.
4. **Broker:** Open at least one account, ideally one without fixed monthly fees to start. FXCM's micro lot CFDs are a good, low-cost way to get started and learn. A second account with a different broker is also worth considering, both for diversification and as a way to manage around the PDT rule.
5. **Scanners:** Most charting software providers (and some broker platforms) include their own scanning tools, so there's no need to rush into an expensive standalone scanner like Trade Ideas early on. **Finviz** offers a solid free scanning service for stocks and ETFs.
6. **Screen capture application:** Free options are widely available.
7. **News services:** Bloomberg and Benzinga offer paid memberships, but their free content is enough to start with. Broker platforms (e.g., IB) typically include good news sources as well. Twitter and Investing.com are both free — just set up a free Twitter account and download the app if not already in use.
