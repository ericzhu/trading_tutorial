## Demo Account vs. Live Trading

A demo account gives access to the broker's platform with virtual funds — the broker credits the account with a set amount of paper money, you trade it until it's run down, and can reset it back to the full virtual amount if needed. You get access to all the platform's functionality, just trading with fake money instead of real cash.

### Why Start on Demo

The first recommendation for any broker platform is to try it out through its demo version first, for two separate reasons:

**1. Master the platform's technical mechanics risk-free.** Losing money because of a "fat finger" mistake, pressing the wrong button, or any other silly slip caused by not knowing the platform well has nothing to do with trading strategy — it's purely a technological/operational risk, and it's entirely avoidable. Before risking a single penny, get comfortable with things like:
- Entering buy/sell orders, finding the trade ticket and trade summary windows, and locating open-position windows and account statements.
- Differentiating between entry and market orders.
- Cancelling, deleting, or amending working orders, and exiting trades.
- Placing trades directly from chart windows: placing and moving stops, and placing limit/take-profit orders (TP1, TP2, TP3, etc.).
- Programming, testing, and using hotkeys.
- Setting up favorite screen layouts, kept as minimal as possible, to maximize the screen space available.

Not all broker platforms are equal, and some are more user-friendly than others — spending time on the demo version is how you'll discover what suits your own trading needs best, and it will save a lot of stress and wasted money from avoidable mistakes.

**2. Practice the strategies learned in the course.** Once comfortable with the pure technical mechanics, the next step is practicing the actual strategies — trading the demo account for at least **three months**, exactly as if it were a real account, applying the same risk and position management rules used in live trading.

Whether three months is too much or too little really depends on the individual and the time dedicated to it, but as a guide: spend enough time to master the platform technically, then enough *additional* time to prove you can make money more or less consistently in the simulated environment. If a strategy can't make money in a demo account, there's no business trading it with real money — nothing about switching to real money would change that outcome.

### Treat Demo Like It's Real

The demo account must be treated exactly as if trading a real account. It's easy to slack off in demo since nothing bad can really happen and it's all virtual — but if genuinely committed and applying the same discipline as with real money, the underlying emotions (greed, fear) involved in everyday trading will still surface to some degree. That said, the single biggest drawback of a demo account is not truly facing your real emotions — the real losses, the real ups and downs, the real greed when deciding what to do with a big winning position — simply because there's no real "skin in the game." The level of real emotion felt is really limited by how seriously/professionally the exercise is approached.

On the positive side, a demo account gives time to practice the craft in a safe environment without losing your shirt — much like an athlete who spends most of their time training and practicing before the real competition. It builds confidence and lets you collect enough data to understand your own performance metrics before going live. This doesn't require thousands of trades — just enough time in simulation to properly analyze trades and identify the key metrics discussed in Module 5:
- **Consistency** — the number of consecutive winning days or weeks achievable.
- **Average profit/loss ratio.**
- **Average win percentage.**

Taking demo training seriously matters because otherwise these metrics won't reflect reality, undermining the confidence needed to start trading real money. It can be frustrating to trade paper money while feeling like real money could have made a killing that month — but just as learning to drive requires time in a semi-simulated environment with an instructor, trading requires this same period of practice to build confidence and collect data. The market will still be there tomorrow, next month, and next year — so there's no rush, and the time spent preparing (without getting paid for it) is simply the cost of tuition. It's a lot cheaper than the losses that would otherwise come from insufficient preparation. The priority above all is **preservation of capital**, so you're still in the game when real opportunities arise and you're fully ready for them.

### Don't Stay in Demo Forever

On the other hand, spending a lifetime in a demo account isn't the goal either — some people get too comfortable there, feeling shy or afraid to "pull the trigger" and go live. Remember the objectives established in Video 1 of this module, and the reason for starting this journey in the first place. Once a solid base has been established in demo trading, it's time to start thinking about putting real skin in the game.

**Transitioning gradually:**
- Don't jump straight from the top floor without a parachute — start slowly, with a small account and small bets, just enough to feel a bit of real pressure with real money on the line, but not so much that it throws off the balance built up during demo practice.
- Scale up size over a few months as confidence builds. Platforms like FXCM offer **micro contracts** with small enough nominal values to let new traders use real money while keeping risk relatively small — covering FX, commodities, stock indices, bonds, etc., at a fraction of the size of regular futures contracts. For stocks, start small with tight dollar-based stops.
- The main goal early on isn't making big money — it's applying the process in real time, with real money, and getting comfortable with it. That's what "having skin in the game" really means, and it builds knowledge and experience.
- Scaling up size later (from, say, 100 shares to 500 to 1,000 to 10,000) is the easy part once the process is properly assimilated and confidence is established. The genuinely difficult part is putting in the real time needed to build that knowledge and experience.

## Keeping a Trading Journal

Once comfortable trading real money and looking to build experience and grow, remember that the beauty of this business is that growth happens every day if you choose it — much like continually working to improve mind, body, and soul in family, work, and life generally. Trade after trade, we learn about ourselves, and hopefully grow as traders by improving our techniques as a result.

This process of tracking your trading journey is called **journaling**, and it's a very important part of the daily trading routine — trades should be entered into the journal at the end of every day, religiously and methodically. It's extra work, and it can feel unpleasant to be reminded of bad trades and losers, but it's essential for genuinely improving strategies and becoming a better trader over time.

### Reviewing and Learning From the Journal

The more interesting part comes when going back to review trades, adding comments and annotations, and studying them at least once a week to draw conclusions about what could have been done better or avoided. Doing this week after week forces a genuine, after-the-fact look at every trade in a safe environment — analyzing what went wrong, what went right, and why.

The most important question to face honestly: **are you willing to learn from your past mistakes (and your good habits)?** Reviewing trades — especially losing ones — regularly and calmly, with the ego set aside, surfaces common recurring mistakes that might otherwise go unnoticed trade after trade. Once there's a decent sample of trades to draw from:
- Identify the recurring mistakes, and write them down somewhere visible near the trading computer as a constant reminder. Making every effort to avoid these repeated errors can meaningfully improve the win/loss ratio and save significant amounts of money.
- On the flip side, identify where the bulk of winning trades come from, and which strategy/setup they belong to.

### Classify Every Trade by Strategy

Every single trade taken should be classified according to a specific pattern or strategy — for example, the core strategies covered earlier in the course (B2B, MRA, etc.). If a trade can't even be labeled with a recognizable pattern or strategy, that's a sign it shouldn't have been taken in the first place — it doesn't fit the strict criteria tied to the patterns actually being traded.

Once trades are classified by pattern, the best-performing setups become statistically obvious — "this is where I make the money, this is where I'm good." There's no need to trade every strategy out there — only the one (or few) that's genuinely well-understood and recognized instantly. With time, experience, and a growing account, there's plenty of opportunity to branch out into other strategies later if desired. Some very successful traders stick to just one or two strategies for their entire careers, simply because it's what makes them money. It's natural to be curious early on and want to try everything (including everything discussed in this course) — that's part of the learning experience for most traders — but ultimately the goal is finding what resonates best personally, with guidance along the way to help narrow that down.

A well-maintained, regularly analyzed trading journal is genuinely powerful: it identifies the biggest flaws and most common mistakes (helping to avoid repeating them), and just as importantly, it identifies real strengths. Simply doing this analysis — knowing what you're good at and bad at — already puts you far ahead of the majority of traders who trade with no strategy, no methodology, and no real understanding of what they do well or poorly.

### Tools for Journaling

**Spreadsheet (Excel):** For years, trades were tracked in a simple Excel spreadsheet — rudimentary, but functional. A typical entry included: instrument, trade date, short/long, entry price, a short description, the broker used, exit date, exit price, profit/loss (in pips, cents, etc.), the initial equity at risk, the profit/loss as a percentage of equity, the final risk/reward ratio, the setup/pattern code or name, and hyperlinks to chart screenshots taken before, during, and sometimes weeks or a month after the trade (useful for revisiting what could have been done better in hindsight). The key isn't sophistication — it's documenting trades with annotations as close to the trade date as possible, while memory of the trade is still fresh.

**Dedicated journaling software:** For those less comfortable building elaborate spreadsheets or coding their own tools, platforms like **TradingView** and **Hedgehog (H1)** do the job even better:
- **TradingView** — used currently; works on a monthly/annual online subscription.
- **Hedgehog (H1)** — being considered as a switch; a lifetime-license, offline software installed on your PC/laptop (pay once, install locally).

Both platforms let you import trades directly from your broker/trading platform — a major time saver over manually filling in a spreadsheet, especially for active traders with multiple day trades to log. TradingView also automatically saves price charts across multiple timeframes for every imported trade — another big time saver compared to manually taking, editing, and pasting screenshots. Both platforms support daily notes, trade notes, tagging trades, sorting by strategy, and — most importantly — offer detailed reports and statistics to help dig deep into your performance.

There's no single "best" platform here — both offer free trials, so it's worth trying each to see which fits best, given their respective pros and cons. A reasonable path: start with the free spreadsheet method at the beginning (it still does the job), then move to TradingView or Hedgehog once serious about leveling up — their cost is small compared to the learning gains and the eventual improvement in maximizing profit and minimizing loss.

## Trading Session Replay: A Middle Ground

Something useful sits between demo trading and journaling: **trading session replay**. These are essentially live replays of past market sessions, playable at any speed — real-time or accelerated/fast-forward — like a DVD capturing and replaying all the market action.

The reason replay fits between demo and journaling: while trading demo, real emotions don't run through the brain quite the way they do live, even with a serious, disciplined mental approach — and everything in a demo session happens live, in real time, same as a real account. Session replay lets you revisit recent past trades after the fact and go through the market action again — similar to rewatching highlights of a football game for a second look and deeper appreciation. This tool is useful both after demo trading and within the context of a real account, since either way, trades happen live in real time — being able to replay recent moves as many times as needed to see what was done right or wrong adds another layer of insight on top of journaling and analyzing those trades.

Many broker platforms now offer this service:
- **FXCM** — across all markets/instruments it covers.
- **TradingView.**
- **TD Ameritrade / thinkorswim** — via their "think back" / "on demand" style replay service.
- **TradingSim** — a dedicated trading replay platform with a very realistic-looking simulated trading environment.

These platforms generally all offer the same core functionality: watching tick-by-tick price changes and simulated trading just like a demo or real account, but using historical data and the ability to fast-forward at multiple speeds. In the end, they all help build practice and test knowledge in a safe environment, aiming toward becoming a better, more experienced trader.

A final, simpler replay option: recording your live screen (charts, Level 2, scanner, etc.) using a screen recorder like **Snagit**, **Camtasia**, or **CyberLink**. Once saved as an MP4 (or equivalent), the recording can be replayed later — though keep in mind this option is for viewing only, not for simulating live trading like the other platforms above.

## Screen Architecture and Broker Platform Setup

Screen space is inherently limited by the number and size of monitors used, and it plays a real role in how quickly needed information can be accessed. The reasoning behind using six monitors in the office setup: keeping all needed information visible directly in front of the eyes, without having to dig through hidden or stacked windows. The essential apps that need to fit somewhere on screen, prioritized front and center, are: charts, the execution platform (at minimum a basic version), a live intraday scanner, and newsfeeds (Twitter and others).

### Example Broker Platform Layout (Interactive Brokers)

Part of maximizing screen space involves placing the broker platform efficiently and using its useful features. On Interactive Brokers, the layout is kept to the minimum number of windows genuinely needed — sometimes hidden in the background when not actively day trading, so clicking any one instantly brings up all of them together. The seven core windows used:

1. **Quote monitor / portfolio (open positions)** — the main, central window, from which account reports, statements, and trade logs are all accessible.
2. **Watchlist monitor** — holding the stocks/FX pairs of the day, or the week's shortlist discussed in the previous module.
3. **Order entry window.**
4. **Time & sales window** — shows every individual print/trade going through the instrument at any given moment, along with each trade's size.
5. **Company-specific news window** (via IB) — automatically shows news related to whichever specific stock is selected.
6. **Options window** — kept visible in preparation for the options trading module (covered in the next chapter).
7. **Chart window** — with pre-programmed hot buttons and hotkeys.

All windows are color-coded into linked groups (using colored chain-link icons) — windows sharing the same color link belong to the same group, and changing the focus/instrument in one "source" window automatically updates every other "destination" window in that group (chart, order entry, etc. all adapt to the newly selected instrument).

**On charting within IB:** the built-in IB charts aren't used for actual technical analysis — **TC2000** remains the charting tool of choice for that. IB's charts are kept open specifically for the convenience of their built-in **hot buttons and hotkeys**, which let take-profit and stop orders be set and adjusted directly from the chart — something TC2000 doesn't offer natively (TC2000 does now offer its own linked execution broker, but the reliability and low transaction costs of IB are preferred, so the two platforms are kept intentionally separate, each used for its own strengths).

IB also provides a decent built-in news platform: general market feeds (Reuters and others), company-specific news linked directly to watchlist symbols, an optional **StockTwits** feed per selected stock (a useful gauge of general trader mood/frenzy around a name for some traders), and even **Bloomberg TV**, all within the same platform.

## Hotkeys and Hot Buttons

Hotkeys and hot buttons (available on IB and most similar platforms, generally offering similar options) are optional extras that can make execution significantly faster — particularly useful for placing buy/sell orders, stop-loss and take-profit orders, and adjusting working orders directly from the chart.

On IB, the **Chart Trader** feature transmits orders instantly with a single click within the chart — button/order defaults are configured from the chart configuration page, then appear on the chart whenever enabled. Clicking arms the cursor to place the selected order type directly onto the chart; once placed, order/position lines appear directly on the chart and can be cancelled or adjusted just by dragging them.

Hotkeys aren't used constantly, and there's no need to go overboard with dozens of them (as some very active scalpers do, for whom execution speed is everything). A simple, personal set typically consists of pre-programmed buy/sell orders, usually limit orders. IB offers a fairly complete hotkey configuration system, letting any keystroke or combination (Control/Shift plus a letter key) be assigned to rapidly create and transmit specific orders to the market.

## Order Types

A quick summary of the main order types used:

- **Limit order:** Buying or selling at a specified price limit (or better). Most entry orders use limit orders. A limit order guarantees the fill won't happen at a price worse than the limit — but it does *not* guarantee a fill at all.
- **Market order:** Buying or selling immediately at the current market bid/offer price. This increases the likelihood and speed of a fill but provides no price protection — the fill could occur meaningfully away from the currently displayed price. Most stop-loss exits (and most exits generally) use market orders.
- **Stop order:** An instruction to submit a buy or sell market order once a specified trigger price is reached or penetrated. A stop order doesn't guarantee a specific execution price and — like a market order — may fill significantly away from the trigger price, unless set up as a **stop-limit** order.
  - A **sell stop** is placed below the current market price — typically to limit loss/protect profit on a long position, or to enter a short on a breakdown below a given level.
  - A **buy stop** is placed above the current market price — typically to limit loss/protect profit on a short position, or to enter long on a breakout above a given level.
  - For breakout/breakdown trades, **stop-limit orders** are generally preferred; for regular entries, plain **limit orders** are used.

These are the core order types in everyday use, though brokers typically offer many more variations — IB alone offers 60+ order types and algos, largely variations on the ones above (beyond the scope here).

## Short Selling: Locates and Borrow Costs

Module 2, Video 1 gave a brief explanation of the short sale process — worth rewatching if it's not fresh in memory. As a quick recap: shorting Avalon at $12.15 and covering at $7.50 produces a profit of $465 before commission (100-share position: a $1,215 credit on the initial sale, a $750 debit on the buy-back). The mechanics: borrow the stock from the broker, sell it in the market, then later buy it back and return the borrowed shares to the broker.

That earlier explanation was intentionally simplified. Here's a fuller look at how borrowing from the broker actually works, and its associated costs.

### The Locate Requirement

To short sell, a **margin account** is required at the brokerage (most traders keep both a cash and a margin account open simultaneously, but a margin account specifically is what's needed for short sales). US regulation requires a broker to have reasonable grounds to believe a security can be borrowed *before* executing any short sale — this is known as the **locate requirement**. Once shares are located/borrowed by the broker, they can be sold in the market, with proceeds deposited into the margin account. Every short sale must be matched to a corresponding locate — though it isn't strictly necessary to secure the locate at the same broker, provided a valid locate can be proven from an identified broker.

### Costs Involved in Shorting

- **Locate fee (borrow fee):** Charged by the broker for borrowing the shares. The size of the fee depends on how difficult the stock is to borrow — the harder it is to borrow, the higher the fee.
- **Collateral requirement:** As the borrower, you must reassure the lender with collateral (cash or treasuries) — sufficient margin in the account to cover the risk of loss if the stock moves against the position.
- **Interest on borrowed margin/cash:** Interest is paid on the margin or cash used as collateral against the borrowed stock. This short interest rate is variable and can change day to day without notice — sometimes quite dramatically during periods of high volatility in a given security.
- **Dividend obligations:** If the company pays a dividend while the stock is being shorted, the short seller is obligated to make up that dividend payment.

These costs are often overlooked but can be substantial, and should be factored directly into a trade's risk/reward calculation before deciding whether the short is still worth taking.

### Modern Locate Process

Many broker platforms now offer automated locate requests. For a stock not on the "easy to borrow" list, there's no longer a need to call a trading desk — everything happens directly within the electronic platform for quicker, easier access to borrows:
1. Identify the stock to short.
2. Send a locate request through the platform for the maximum number of shares needed, either before market open or before the trade.
3. Accepting the locate for a hard-to-borrow stock triggers the locate fee immediately — even if the located security is never actually shorted.
4. Holding a located hard-to-borrow position **overnight** typically triggers an additional **overnight fee**, usually calculated as a multiple (2x, 3x, sometimes 5x or more) of the locate fee. For example, a $50 locate fee held overnight could result in $150–$250+ in total charges.

Some brokers — **TradeZero** is a notable, fast-growing name in this space — now let traders **sell back** unused or partially used locates (e.g., after finishing trading for the day, or deciding not to use them), helping to recoup some of the initial locate cost.

### CFDs and Options as Alternatives

The entire locate/borrow process described above doesn't apply to a **CFD** account (contracts for difference, offered by brokers like **IG Index** or **Saxo Bank**), since a CFD is a derivative product based on an underlying stock rather than a physical share sale — no borrowing or locating required. The same applies to buying **put options** instead of shorting stock directly, avoiding this whole process.

However, keep in mind the trade-offs:
- CFD brokers don't allow trading every stock, particularly in the OTC/small-cap category.
- Not every stock has listed options either.
- Since no physical stock changes hands, what's actually held is a derivative position with the broker or exchange as the counterparty — meaning CFDs and options both carry an extra layer of **counterparty risk** compared to trading the physical stock.
