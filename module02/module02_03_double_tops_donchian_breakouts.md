## Double Tops and Double Bottoms

**First example:** a clean double bottom forms, the market reacts, consolidates briefly, and starts moving up again.

**Follow-up on the engulfing example from the previous video:** the double bottom we saw there also lined up with major support from the 200-day moving average — price returns to the prior low around 38.35 and starts moving back up. When a double bottom, the 200-day moving average, and an engulfing pattern all line up at the same level, that convergence gives us good probabilities on a long trade.

**XYZ-type example:** another double bottom, with the market bouncing off the 200-day moving average at a level that also corresponds to the low made back in February.

**A second stock ("X²"):** shows both a double top and a double bottom. Price first finds support on the 50-day moving average, bounces up to a top, and forms a double top dating back to June. It then drops back down at the end of July, bounces off the previously established bottom, and forms a double bottom — though in this case price also broke through the 50-day moving average, making it a more daring trade. Still, it illustrates how double tops/bottoms can help set targets or entry levels.

**XLU (Utilities):** a double bottom forms in May and June, with lows around 48.70 and 47.50 (about a dollar apart) — and in fact the same 48.60 price zone had already held before, making it effectively a triple bottom. Each time, the market reacted strongly and rallied.

**EFCOM (Telecom ETF):** a double bottom formed in September 2015 and January 2016, bouncing from the exact same low both times before rallying. On the upside, the same ETF hit the same resistance level four times (effectively a triple top) before finally stopping just short of it, consolidating, and later breaking through and rallying.

**XLI (Industrial Sector ETF, weekly):** a double bottom around 48.30 coincided with a convergence of signals — Donchian channels and the 200-week moving average (200 weeks ≈ 1,000 trading days) — all pointing to strong support. This kind of convergence (200-period moving average + double bottom) is a high-probability setup. The same chart also shows a possible double top forming near the same resistance, with price breaking new lows below the 10- and 20-week moving averages.

**Another weekly example ("Excel"):** price hits the same resistance area three times, forming a double top on the second touch (around 78.48), pulling back just below the 50-week moving average, consolidating, rallying, and hitting resistance a third time — becoming sandwiched between the 50-week and 200-week moving averages and this triple-top resistance.

**A final example on an individual stock** (moving beyond ETFs — a reminder that sector ETFs offer diversification and lower volatility for less aggressive traders who don't want single-stock risk, and that a portfolio can be built entirely from ETFs using this methodology): price bounces off support multiple times, nearly breaks the old high at 667 but fails, forming a weekly double top before the market collapses. On the daily chart, price gaps open above the old top, immediately forms an engulfing pattern, bounces briefly off the 20-day moving average, then collapses again. When you see an engulfing pattern that also looks like a double top, be very careful.

## Donchian Channels

Donchian channels were created in the early 1950s by Richard Donchian, a veteran stock and futures trader later known as the "father of trend following." His ideas influenced market wizards like Ed Seykota, Richard Dennis, and Bill Eckhardt — the latter two used this methodology as a core component of what became known as the Turtle Trading system (a story for another day).

**Definition:** a Donchian channel is an indicator formed by an upper and lower band surrounding price. The upper band marks the highest price over the last *n* periods (candlesticks); the lower band marks the lowest price over the same *n* periods.

**Periods used on the daily chart:** 5, 21, 63, 126, and 252 — corresponding to 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of trading. These give reference points for whether a stock is about to break the high or low it established 5 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months, or 12 months ago. They're not necessarily all used at once, but they generate ideas, targets, and — especially — inputs for breakout setups (discussed next).

**Periods used on a 15-minute chart** (for fine-tuning entries): 26 and 52, corresponding to the high/low of the last one and two trading days — the most relevant reference points for short-term/day trading.

**Periods used on the weekly chart:** 26 and 52, corresponding to the high/low established six months and one year ago.

These tools are part of the overall toolkit of the Trading Academy methodology — pieces of a larger puzzle that will come together, along with risk and money management, once the full methodology is covered.

## Breakout Setups

A breakout is the moment a stock breaks through a resistance or support level to the upside or downside. As discussed earlier, resistance and support levels typically reflect a "wall" of sellers or buyers at a specific price. If buyers aren't strong enough in number or buying power to overcome that wall, resistance holds and price stops advancing. A breakout occurs when buying power finally becomes strong enough — usually accompanied by a large volume spike — to overwhelm that wall of sellers, tipping the demand/supply balance and pushing price higher (or the reverse, for a breakdown).

### Successful Breakout Examples

**Low-priced stock example (from ~$2.50):** after a long downtrend (price under the 50- and 200-day moving averages, Donchian channels stepping down) and three months of consolidation between $1.75 and $2.50, a massive volume spike (~11–12x the 20-day average) hits. Day one fails to make a new high above $2.50; day two shows price approaching the 10/20/50 moving averages and the important 200-day average without breaking $2.50; day three brings another huge volume spike, breaking above both $2.50 and the 200-day moving average. After a brief consolidation at the 200-day average (acting as initial resistance), the stock rallies hard — one day prints ~4 million shares versus a ~330,000 average (11–12x) — running from $2.50 to $5.75, pulling back to the 10/20/200-day averages, then resuming the uptrend all the way to $8.51 (from an original breakout around $2.50). A later breakout above the $8 resistance level (also flagged by the Donchian bands) leads to a gap-up open near $11.50–$12, with the stock trading well above its 10/20/50 averages, eventually reaching $36 before correcting back below the moving averages.

**JVO-type example:** after a long downtrend, one day prints 30 million shares versus a 20-day average of 1.7 million (~30x average volume). Price breaks through the 10/20/50 moving averages and the 21-day Donchian channel (a new monthly high above $8), then rallies all the way to $24.75 — precisely the level flagged by the Donchian channel as a target, alongside other profit targets around $14 and $19 identified from longer-term Donchian levels. This illustrates how breakouts above moving averages and Donchian channels (marking 1/3/6/9/12-month highs) combine to define both entries and profit/stop targets.

**Docker-type example:** a low-volume stock consolidating around $3.50 sees a news-driven volume surge (564,000 vs. a 29,000 average), breaking through several Donchian channels (5-day, 6-month) and the 10/20/50/200 moving averages. The first Donchian target near $5.80 (also near the 200-day average) offers a scale-out point. The next day, volume jumps to nearly 3 million (vs. ~30,000 average), with price opening around $6.50, spiking to $8.50, then settling below the prior close.

**XR-type example:** price breaks the 126-day (6-month) Donchian high but stalls at the 252-day (12-month) high — a near-miss rather than a clean breakout. After three days of consolidation holding above the breakout level and the 10/20/50/200 moving averages, a massive volume day (2.8 million vs. a rising ~327,000 average) pushes higher, followed by an even bigger volume day (18.6 million shares) that finally clears the 12-month high at $9.30 — sending the stock from $9.29 to almost $22 in a single day, and from $5 to $22 in two days.

**Arcadia (Basic Materials):** in a downtrend under the 50/200-day moving averages, price consolidates between $4 and $6 from December 2017 to March, with resistance around $6.12–$6.60. A first attempt near $7 fails despite elevated volume (1.3 million vs. a 90,000 average) — a classic failed breakout. Price then breaks above the 50-day moving average but not the prior high. After further consolidation, another high-volume day (~1 million vs. 150,000 average, 9x) finally clears the $7.50 high (site of the earlier failed breakout) and the 200-day moving average, sending price from ~$6 to $17 in two days. The advance later stalls at the 252-day Donchian high (12-month high) near $18–19, pulls back to $12, then resumes — holding above the 10-day moving average and moving VWAP — to reach $65: a roughly 10x move from the original ~$7 breakout in just four trading days (700% gain).

**Ultra-low-volume "story" stock example:** a stock that normally trades almost no volume suddenly prints 2.2 million shares (average jumps from ~500–700 to 110,000) on a news catalyst, breaking through all moving averages (10/20/50/200) and all Donchian channels (1/6/9/12-month highs), rocketing from under $3 to $45 in a single day — before reverting to its prior dormant state within a few days. These plays require very strict risk and money management given their extreme volatility and short-lived nature.

**ORCS-type example:** a very low-volume stock (10,000–20,000 average) ranges between $2 and $3.50 through 2017–2018, mostly inside the Donchian bands. Two earlier attempts near $2.50 show elevated but not extreme volume and fail to clear the $3.60 high (intraday plays only). A final breakout — with massive volume (~11 million vs. a 700,000 average, ~15x) — clears both $2.50 and the $3.20/$3.40 prior highs, ultimately reaching $4.50: a 350% advance in four days. Comparing the failed attempts to the successful one highlights the difference: big, fat green candles pushing decisively through resistance and Donchian levels with massive volume each time are the setups worth swing trading. These stocks can also give back gains just as fast — in this case, a 10x move from $2 to $20 over five days reversed completely back to $2 by day five, underscoring the need for strict risk and money management.

**InBev-type example:** after grinding in a range, an early attempt above the 21-day (1-month) Donchian high near $2.30–2.50 fails at the 200-day moving average. A later breakdown attempt below $1.60 also offers limited profit potential. The eventual valid setup is a breakout above $2.60 (where price was previously stopped and where the 200-day moving average sits) — this time with massive volume (51 million vs. a 5.5–6 million average, ~10x), sending price from ~$2 to $10 (400% in four days) before stalling at the round-number resistance of $10, forming a near double top.

**A clean downside breakout example:** a breakdown below $3.50 initially shows very low volume (under 300,000) and isn't compelling; a second down day shows only slightly higher volume. After a long, dormant consolidation (50,000–150,000 average volume), a major catalyst hits: volume explodes to 22 million (vs. 1.2 million average, ~20x), then 63 million the next day, pushing price through all Donchian channels and the 10/20/50 moving averages toward the 200-day average and a gap-up open near the round number of $6 — which also aligns with a 61.8% Fibonacci retracement near $5.50 (Fibonacci retracement will be covered later as another convergence signal). The stock moves from under $1 to $6 in two days.

**A boss-type downside breakout example:** price ranges between $8.65 and $12.60, then breaks down below $8.65 with a large volume spike (3.8 million vs. a 400,000 average, ~10x) via a big red candle, falling to $4 within three days.

**IGC (penny stock):** trading around $0.50 with occasional spikes to $1–$1.75. A breakout above the Donchian level of $0.68 is followed by a volume spike and a strong green candle clearing resistance and moving averages, then an even bigger spike drives the stock from $0.50 to $2 (300%) in four days. After a pullback that fails to clear the 10/20/50 moving averages, another breakout above the $2.05 Donchian high — confirmed by a huge volume day (76 million vs. a 15 million average) — carries price to $4.25, and ultimately to $14.70 (600% over six days) before collapsing back to $4.

**STM-type example:** a breakout above the 252-day (12-month) Donchian high occurs on decent but unexceptional volume (3.5 million vs. ~2 million+ average) and doesn't immediately take off; after consolidating back to the 10/20-day moving averages, a much bigger volume day (21 million vs. a 3.3 million average, ~7x) finally produces the intended leg higher — illustrating the kind of volume-confirmed breakout worth taking.

**Amara-type example:** a dormant, flat-ranging stock ($2.80–$4.50, with its own double top/bottom) suddenly gaps up from $3 to above $10 on major news, rallying to $21 over eight trading sessions (400%+). Gap breakouts that open extremely far above the prior close (here, 240% above) are riskier and less preferred, though outcomes depend heavily on the catalyst and existing short interest — there's no firm rule, but the technical setup should still confirm the usual parameters and filters.

**GBR-type example (closing example):** repeated breakout attempts near $1.82 fail multiple times, with price returning to its prior range — until the market finally breaks decisively, running from $2 to $13. A later setup targets a breakout above $2.60; price reaches $4.50 before retreating — a trade better suited to intraday/day trading given the risk/reward on that specific move.
