## Technical Analysis vs. Fundamental Analysis

There are two main ways of looking at markets: technical and fundamental. I am not a fundamental trader — I trade what I see based on price action and technical analysis only. I've tried trading off fundamental research in the past without success. That doesn't mean fundamental analysis is worthless; it just never worked well for me personally. I do keep fundamental data in mind (economic releases, earnings reports, company news), but the bulk of my trading methodology is technical.

**Fundamental analysis** studies the *cause* of market movement — supply, demand, and economic statistics that inform a buy or sell decision.

**Technical analysis** studies the *effect* — it's the study of market price, action, and volume, primarily through charts, for the purpose of forecasting future price trends.

Both approaches try to solve the same problem — where price is likely to go next — just from different directions.

### Why Technical Analysis? Three Core Premises

Without believing in each of the following three premises, technical analysis wouldn't hold up on its own.

**1. Price discounts everything.**
Anything that can affect price — fundamentally or psychologically — is already reflected in the current price. Price action is all that's required; it reflects the shift in supply and demand (if demand exceeds supply, price rises, and vice versa). Charts don't cause markets to move; they simply reflect the bullish or bearish psychology behind the move. So we don't concern ourselves with *why* — we study price charts and let the market tell us which way it's most likely to go. The market is stronger than any of us, so don't fight it — embrace it.

**2. Price moves in trends.**
The whole purpose of technical analysis is to identify trends in their early stages. This mirrors Newton's law of motion: an object in motion stays in motion until acted upon by an unbalanced force. Once a market is trending, it tends to continue until it reverses. Our job is to detect the point of inflection and jump on/off the trend — not to pick tops and bottoms.

  - Example: the S&P 500 has been in a powerful bull market since bottoming in March 2009 — nearly a decade of uninterrupted uptrend.
  - Example: Bitcoin rallied from roughly $1 (2009) to $1,000, then exploded from ~$1,000 to ~$20,000 in 2017, before settling into a range around $6,000–$7,000. Both examples illustrate how powerful and long-lasting trends can be, and why fighting them is dangerous.

**3. History tends to repeat itself.**
Price ultimately reflects human psychology, and behavioral finance shows that greed and fear have driven human behavior the same way for centuries, producing recurring boom-and-bust cycles under different names — the credit crunch, the tech bubble, the crypto craze, Ponzi schemes, and so on. Market prices act as a leading indicator of fundamentals, since known fundamentals are already discounted. As Keynes said, "the market can remain irrational a lot longer than you can stay solvent." This is also why technical analysis offers more precise, tangible entry/exit points than fundamental analysis, which tends to be vaguer on timing.

Jesse Livermore's take reinforces this: traders repeatedly act out of greed, fear, ignorance, and hope, which is why chart patterns recur. It's often futile to chase the "reason" behind a move — the real reason may not surface until well after the move has happened, and by the time news is public, most of the move has already occurred ("buy the rumor, sell the fact"). Chasing reasons costs focus and discipline; better to simply notice the stock is moving and react. As Livermore put it: "There's nothing new on Wall Street... whatever happens in the stock market today has happened before and will happen again."

These three premises explain why many traders — including myself — favor technical analysis over the efficient market theory (EMT) as the practical approach to markets. Not everyone will agree, and that's fine — it's important to understand where you stand relative to other approaches.

## Long and Short Positions

There are two ways to position in the market:

### Going Long
Buying an asset with the expectation it will rise in value.

*Example — Cronos Group:* Buy above $8.15, stop-loss $0.60 below entry. Take partial profits at $10.15 and move the stop to breakeven. Bought low, sold high — a typical long trade.

### Going Short (Short Selling)
Selling an asset first, with the expectation of buying it back later at a lower price — the reverse of a long position.

*Example — Avalon Holdings:* Sell short below $12.15, with a wide stop at $15.55 (given the stock's volatility). Cover (buy back) below $7.50, then take a partial profit and move the stop to breakeven.

On a 100-share position: selling short at $12.15 generates a $1,215 credit; buying back at $7.50 costs a $750 debit — a gross profit of $465 before commissions.

**Mechanics of a short sale:** the trader borrows shares from the broker, sells them into the market, later buys them back at a lower price, and returns the borrowed shares to the broker — realizing the difference as profit (or loss). In forex and futures, a short position can be opened at any time; in the stock market, the extra step of borrowing shares makes shorting slightly more complex.

**Risk asymmetry:** going long has theoretically unlimited upside (a stock can go from $1 to $100+) but the loss is capped at 100% of the position. Shorting is the mirror image — the maximum profit is capped (the stock can only fall to zero), while the potential loss is unlimited if the stock keeps rising. Because of this, shorting is inherently more speculative and dangerous, even though markets often fall faster than they rise. Always respect this risk asymmetry when shorting.

## The Dow Theory

Charles Dow founded the Dow Jones Company in 1882. He never wrote a book on his theory; his ideas were published as newspaper articles in the Wall Street Journal in 1903 (the year after his death) and later compiled by Nelson, who coined the term "Dow Theory." Its basic tenets:

1. **The average discounts everything** — stock prices quickly incorporate new information as it becomes available.

2. **The market has three movements**, compared by Dow to tides, waves, and ripples:
   - *Primary trend (tide)*: lasts a year or more.
   - *Secondary/intermediate trend (wave)*: a correction within the primary trend, lasting three weeks to three months, typically retracing one-third to two-thirds (commonly about half) of the prior move.
   - *Minor trend (ripple)*: lasts less than three weeks, occurring within the intermediate trend.

3. **Trends have three phases**:
   - *Accumulation*: smart investors buy/sell against the prevailing market opinion; price barely moves since they're a minority absorbing supply.
   - *Public participation*: the broader market catches on, price moves rapidly, and trend followers (technical traders like myself) join in.
   - *Distribution*: after massive speculation, the smart money begins exiting to the market.

4. **The averages must confirm each other** — in Dow's era, no major bull/bear signal was valid unless both the industrial and rail averages agreed.

5. **Volume must confirm the trend.**

6. **A trend remains in effect until a definite reversal signal appears** — the market can temporarily move against the trend, but the trend deserves the benefit of the doubt until proven otherwise. Determining whether a move is a genuine reversal or a temporary pullback is inherently difficult, and Dow theorists often disagree.

### Defining Trend and Reversal

Dow defined an **uptrend** as a sequence of successive rally highs and lows that are each higher than the ones before; a **downtrend** is the mirror image. Dow relied exclusively on *closing* prices — an average had to close above the previous peak (or below the previous trough) to be significant; intraday penetration didn't count. Many modern traders, however, do use intraday levels when applying Dow theory.

**Failure swing vs. non-failure swing:** In a failure swing, price makes a higher low but fails to make a new high above the prior peak (point A), then breaks below the prior low (point B) — a sell signal (at point S). In a non-failure swing, price does make a new high above point A, then corrects to a new low below point B (point D), bounces (point E), and ultimately continues lower (point F). Traders disagree on which break constitutes the actual sell signal.

*Example — EUR/USD:* Price bottoms near 120, rallies to point A (fails to clear ~138), pulls back to point B, then breaks above A on the next rally, confirming a trend change and climbing to point C (~130 area retracement). It then holds a higher low than point B, fails to break down near 129 (point D), and rallies to a higher high near 149 (point E) — a textbook sequence of higher highs and higher lows.

## Support and Resistance

Price often runs into resistance that blocks further advances until broken; once broken, that old resistance tends to become new support (and vice versa on the way down).

I personally favor **horizontal** trend lines over diagonal ones — they're cleaner to draw and less subjective, since diagonal lines depend heavily on which highs/lows you choose to connect.

**Convergence zones ("price clusters"):** support and resistance are rarely a single exact price — they're better thought of as a zone where multiple horizontal levels converge. The more confluence you find, and the higher the timeframe, the more reliable that zone tends to be as a bounce point. This concept also helps deal with false breakouts. Round numbers (e.g., 10, 50, 200) often act as natural resistance/support levels.

*Example — Lulu (LULU):* Through most of Q1 2018, price was range-bound between $75 and $85, testing the $85 resistance five times before finally breaking through on strong volume — after which it entered a sustained bull run. Resistance levels like this tend to form for three reasons:
1. Existing long holders selling out at that level.
2. Traders shorting at a level that has already held once before.
3. Stressed buyers who bought near/above resistance getting out near their entry (breakeven) level.

A second example near $130 shows the same pattern: repeated rejection, moving averages capping the rally, then a breakout on high volume followed by a large gap day — confirming the shift from resistance to established uptrend.

## Trading Styles

**Swing trading** — detecting a trend and positioning long or short with a medium-to-long-term view. Positions may be held for 3–5 days, weeks, or even months if the trend persists. Dow theory typically underlies this style of analysis and positioning.

**Day trading (intraday trading)** — positioning with a short-term view, exiting before the session ends. Holding periods range from 30 minutes to several hours depending on momentum. The logic is similar to swing trading but compressed into a single session. Most day traders end the day flat, though a successful intraday trade can occasionally be converted into a swing trade if the setup shows potential beyond the daily range.

**Scalping** — an intense style involving many ultra-short-term trades (100+ per day is possible), targeting small moves (roughly $0.10–$0.20 on stocks, or 10–20 pips in forex) over a holding period of seconds to a few minutes. Position sizes can be larger than in swing trading given the tight risk management, but the underlying asset must be liquid and volatile enough to support the turnover. Sudden news events causing price gaps make scalping riskier for larger positions — always be mindful of leverage. As with day trades, a successful scalp can occasionally be extended into an intraday trade if the potential seems to extend beyond the original scalping timeframe.
