## Top-Down Sector Ranking

The way I like to conduct this study is to look at relative strength analysis of the top sectors. I have my top sectors on my scanner/charting software (TC2000), and I look at the gainers and losers over a specific time frame — it could be a year-to-date ranking, a five-day ranking, or a change-from-close ranking, depending on the time frame you want to work with and are most comfortable with.

That's really what relative strength is: comparing the close of a certain price with the close of the same instrument's price X days ago — one year ago, year-to-date, five days ago, or the last close. We look at the top gainers and top losers of the list.

**What are sectors?** Part of the S&P 500 will be ranked into industries and sectors, and every sector is a combination of all the companies operating within it. So a sector index or ETF isn't tracking just one company — it gathers many different companies that are part of that sector. We're not aiming at one company only, but at what the sector as a whole is doing.

So the first step is to find out who's been the top gainer and top loser over a specific time frame. This gives us an idea of where we want to be and which sector we want to look at, because I never want to be in a choppy market — one that's been trading in a range, doing nothing. I want to trade the markets, stocks, and sectors that have been actively trading and showing favor among investors. These markets will either be the top gainers, attracting a lot of investors who want to buy, or the top losers, in which case I'd be looking to short them because they're showing dislike from the investing community.

This is where rotation comes from — rotating from one sector or industry into another. Institutional money, funds, mutual funds, insurance companies start liking one sector and disliking another, and the rest of us jump on the bandwagon, trying to make money trading on the back of those flows, momentum, and trends.

The idea is the same as what we've seen in the previous two videos — we look at sectors through multiple time frame analysis. But the first step is finding out which sectors are gaining the most and which are losing the most. Then, once we've analyzed the top sectors, we look at the components — since a sector index is a combination of many different companies (some big, some small), we approach it from a top-down perspective: from the sector-based ETF or index, down to singling out individual stocks.

So the process is: first find the gainers and losers among the sectors we're interested in, then within those sectors look at the ETF/sector itself (composed of many stocks), and finally go through the individual stocks trying to find the single stock, part of that sector, that shows the right convergence with the sector and the perfect setup out of all the stocks making up that sector index.

## Building a Diversified Portfolio with ETFs

Using ETFs (Exchange Traded Funds) is a way to build an active, diversified core portfolio for more conservative active investors. This part of the course is geared more towards conservative active investors. If you came to this course, it's because you were interested in taking over your finances and portfolios, making your own decisions rather than depending on brokers or private bankers.

But we're not all the same type of investor. Some are active traders like myself — though I don't consider myself an active trader compared to guys who scalp all day; I consider myself an active trader/investor, but definitely not the most active. I also relate to friends who trade their portfolios on a weekly basis, taking a swing-trading approach: looking at their portfolios to make a few active changes now and then, but definitely not looking at daily price action.

ETFs are a good way for investors who aren't willing to be stuck to their screen, checking quotes every other minute. You can build a nicely diversified portfolio in terms of both risk and asset types — stocks, other asset classes like bonds, commodities, different types of strategies.

We used to have only mutual funds, most of them long-only, and years ago we didn't have many choices. Since the advent of ETFs, we now have the possibility of investing across all sorts of asset classes, popular strategies, regions, and market types — and in most cases, actively. I'm looking at US ETFs trading on the US stock market because that's where you'll find the most assets under management and the most liquidity.

You get a liquid stock — instead of buying one single company, you buy an ETF composed of many different stocks or bonds, but at the end of the day it's liquid and already diversified. When you build a portfolio with these ETF building blocks across different assets and strategies, you end up with a diversified portfolio made up of many thousands of different securities.

That doesn't mean this portfolio stays stagnant. The methodology I'm teaching you is applicable across all time frames, instruments, and portfolios — so you'll have your core set of chosen ETFs, but you'll apply the same rules for when to go long, when to go short, or when to simply hold cash.

### A Note on Shorting

Some people don't like going short, and I'm not a huge fan of it either — there's a lot of risk in shorting, especially single stocks. The risk is a lot more elevated: a stock might announce a big piece of news or a merger overnight, gap up 500-800%, and if you're short you can literally obliterate your account. That's usually why I'm not a super big fan of shorting single stocks — if I'm shorting intraday, that's one thing, but I consider my risk very strictly.

When I want to short the overall market, I'd rather play that through an ETF or CFD on a general index (S&P, Nasdaq, etc.) rather than a single stock. There's no chance an index will open the next day with a 500%, 100%, or even 20% gap — it just doesn't happen. It might gap 2, 3, or 5% overnight, which is already enormous, but your exposure being short is not the same as with a single stock.

Same thing with FX — the most liquid market out there. Even if the market gaps against you, you'll find massive liquidity, and it's extremely rare to see gaps of 50%, 20%, or even 10% — we've never seen that kind of move even during Brexit.

So, an ETF core portfolio brings diversification and lets you trade long or short. Trading ETFs on the short side is less risky than shorting single stocks. Some people, when their multi-time-frame analysis leads them to conclude that a particular asset class/ETF is heading down, simply decide to sell off the position and remain in cash instead of shorting.

## What Kind of Trader Do You Want to Be?

Before going into the top-down analysis of sectors and components, this brings us back to a question we've tackled before: what sort of trader or active investor do you want to be? This is very personal.

- **Day trading**: taking a position and making sure you come back home flat, no position whatsoever, so you can sleep comfortably with no stress. This is more intense — you need to be in front of your screen and reasonably active, though "active" doesn't mean hundreds of trades a day (we covered in the psychology module why sticking to two or three trades a day is important).
- **Swing trading**: taking a position that might start intraday but carrying part or all of it — possibly having secured some profit or moved the stop to breakeven — overnight and over multiple days, depending on how the position develops. This is a slower-paced type of trading/investing: you can look at the market close, decide what you want to do based on your methodology, put your orders and stop-losses in the next day, and not need to watch it constantly.

This is a good approach for people who don't want to stay glued to their screen all day monitoring positions — they can do their analysis at the end of the day, put their orders in when the market opens, and go about their day. So the main question is what sort of trader/active investor you want to be — and only by delving into the methodology and starting to place some trades will you get a clue of where you'd like to go.

## Doing the Analysis: Sector → Industry → Stock (TC2000 Walkthrough)

I've used TC2000 for a while — there are a lot of really good charting platforms out there (TradingView, ProRealTime, the one that comes with E*TRADE, etc.), but one thing I really appreciate about TC2000 is the work they've put into sector/sectoral analysis, which makes performing sector rotation analysis a lot easier.

My screen layout: the main chart in the center, a window listing the **sector indices**, and a window with **industries**, plus a selected list of **SPDR and Fidelity sector ETFs** — the most active, representative ETFs for holding sector exposure in a portfolio (again, composed of many stocks part of that sector).

**Step 1 — Rank sectors.** The sector index window lists the main sectors (technology, healthcare, energy, industrials, utilities, etc.), ranked from most positive to most negative on the day versus the prior close. In the example, technology had been acting very badly — clicking on it brings up the technology sector chart in the center window. It had been doing extremely well until recently, breaking through the 10- and 20-week moving averages, all the way down to the 50-week moving average, holding briefly before trading below it — the trend was definitely changing. On the daily chart, same story: trading below the 50-day moving average (never a good sign), breaking through Donchian support and prior price supports, establishing a new low/lower high — it doesn't look good.

**Step 2 — Rank industries within the sector.** The technology sector is made up of sub-components called industries (shown in the window below): solar, information technology, electronic gaming, health information services, etc. Ranking by industry from worst to best performer on that day, consumer electronics and various semiconductor sub-industries (semiconductors, memory chips, equipment, etc.) were the worst performers.

**Step 3 — Scan the sub-industry charts for the cleanest setup.** Some charts are cleaner than others — I'm looking for nice convergence, not a market stuck in a range. A "contract manufacturers" industry chart looked really nice: trend in convergence with the sector (moving down), price comfortably below the moving averages, nice parallel moving averages with a steep angle. That's the kind of chart pattern I want — something that lines up with the industry/sector's own trend (which had broken down, gone through the 200-day moving average, bounced against it, moved back down, closed below the 50-week MA on the weekly, same long-term trend change on the daily). I'm looking for something with a lot of downward convergence that's close to a breakout or close to a moving average where I know we'll find resistance — a good entry point for shorting.

**Step 4 — Drill into individual stocks within the chosen industry.** Going back to the actual numbers: technology sector was down -3.23% on the day, and within technology, the "contract manufacturers" industry was down about -2.97% (almost 3%). The right-hand window lists all the components of that sub-industry (ICS code 2390 — the S&P code for contract manufacturers, part of the technology sector) — companies like Sigmatron, Flex, Celestica (Altec/Team Technology-type names), etc.

Ranking those stocks from top losers to top gainers on that day: most stocks in the industry were down, apart from one that was bucking the trend big time — interesting, because sometimes a stock or sector acts totally against the main chain of the market/sector, and that's often also a very good play (we'll cover this more in later modules). In general it's better/safer to trade a stock that goes along with the main direction of the market/sector, but when you see a stock trading on higher-than-average volume and breaking out massively on the daily chart (first through $3, then through the $3.18 level, on massive volume, bucking the sector/market trend), that's a good indication there are strong/institutional hands accumulating the stock for some reason — sometimes tied to a real news catalyst (which adds credibility to the move), sometimes to shareholder actions.

If instead I want a stock showing nice convergence with the industry/sector trend (not one bucking it), I go through the list of components looking for the cleanest chart:
- One stock had no volume whatsoever, stuck in a range — doesn't make the cut at all.
- Another showed a nice daily downtrend matching the sector/industry, with a clean breakout, continuing to move down — I'd look for another breakout, or a bounce back against one of the main moving averages (a clean trending stock, up or down, tends to be contained by the 10- or 20-day moving average).
- JBL: not bad, but the trend/bounce had been very short.
- Another had great convergence with the downward trend, but had already gapped down hard at the open, so a lot of the move had already played out — could look for a bounce against the 10-day MA back toward the prior low established since a specific date, but trading in the main direction of the downtrend increases the probabilities.
- Another had an elastic/scaled-up chart — not a favorite.
- **TDM Technology**: a nice trend, a clean breakout just short of $17, trending below the 10/20-day moving averages and below VWAP — everything lining up. This, along with Sigmatron, were the two interesting candidates found in this pass.

**Live market-open example.** (Recording was done just before the market opened, then continued after open — quotes update accordingly.) Ranking sectors top gainers to top losers after the open, technology was now the top-performing sector. Drilling into the sub-industries within technology, "semiconductor equipment" was the top performer, up about 1.5% on the day — but the chart pattern wasn't inspiring; the trend was still down, not trading off any consistent support (down heavily for the prior three days, so the bounce could just be short covering) — not a place to start buying. If I wanted to buy, I'd rank the individual stocks in that industry from top gainers to top losers and look for one coming off a nice convergence of support levels before considering an entry via my methodology.

## Building an ETF-Based Sector Portfolio

For those more interested in holding diversified sector exposure rather than single stocks (e.g., if you're only comfortable holding big caps, or prefer a diversified basket), you can build a portfolio of 3-5 top sectors according to the methodology, using sector ETFs instead of single stocks — that's why I created a dedicated watchlist of SPDR and Fidelity sector ETFs (the two main providers of the most actively traded sector ETFs). Depending on your broker, you can sometimes switch between sector ETFs within the same family commission-free (e.g., Fidelity), though commissions aren't as big an issue these days.

This watchlist mirrors the sector index window, except it shows the ETF versions (tracker funds following the main sectors). Ranking by today's change from last night's close: SPDR S&P Semiconductor & Semiconductor Equipment ETF matched what we'd seen with the technology/semiconductor equipment industry; also shown are Invesco QQQ (tracking the Nasdaq index, one of the most/if-not-the-most active ETFs by assets under management and volume) and the SPDR Technology Sector ETF (aiming at the broader sector, as opposed to a narrower industry).

If you wanted to diversify a portfolio even further — beyond sectors, on an asset-class basis — you'd add other asset classes to this list: fixed income (Treasury bonds, European bonds, government/corporate bonds, junk bonds, etc.), commodity ETFs, and real estate ETFs (real estate is widely considered — including by me — an asset class in its own right). You could diversify by region too (European stock indices, or even global sectors, since iShares also provides ETFs tracking sectors on a global basis rather than just the US market, pulling in major caps from German, French, Italian, Spanish, Indian, etc. markets within a given sector).

The combinations are quite varied and multiple — but the point is to give you a taste of what's possible using our core strategies and multi-time frame analysis, whether you day trade or swing trade single stocks. We want to see maximum multi-time convergence among the different time units we're looking at, and entry points that answer to our strategy and methodology. But we can apply exactly the same methodology to ETFs and to a much less active portfolio — reviewed weekly, biweekly, or monthly, with a lot less turnover but the exact same process for picking the top ETF sectors/asset classes.

## Yearly Sector Performance Example

Using the same sector/industry index windows, but this time ranking by yearly performance: ranking 2017 from top performer to top loser, the technology sector was up 34% that year — the top performer of 2017. Drilling into the industries making up technology for that year: semiconductor memory, electronic gaming, and others were the top performers — semiconductor memory was up 72% for the year. Drilling into the companies making up that industry: the top company was up almost 250% in 2017; the second-best was Micron, whose 2017 trend was absolutely amazing.

This is useful for getting a sense of things on a yearly basis, especially around corrective years. In the last two negative years for the S&P (2011 and 2015), the only sectors that were top performers both times were healthcare, consumer defensive, and utilities. (2015 didn't include utilities, likely because that year saw a rally in the bond market, and utilities tend to trade closely with interest rates.) Then there's 2008, a total washout — no matter what you were invested in that year, there was a good probability you ended up losing money, because most sectors were down.
