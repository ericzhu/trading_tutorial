## Why US Stocks and ETFs

I'm based in Europe, so in the morning I like to trade some foreign exchange pairs and some commodities, but in the afternoon I start focusing on stock trading — specifically US stocks, including ETFs. Why the focus on US stocks and the US exchange? Remember what we covered in previous modules about the advantages, disadvantages, and particular strengths of US markets compared to others: the bulk of global volume and liquidity is still massively centered in the US. In Module 1 we introduced the concept of the size and liquidity of the US exchange compared to other exchanges — I invite you to review that again and look at the different stats; I'm sure you'll be persuaded, as I was, that it's the best market.

That said, I don't want to drive you away from your home market. You might be Spanish, French, German, whatever — you might have a home bias and be inclined to trade your own local stocks and companies, and I'm not disputing that at all. I'm simply telling you that after years of experience across exchanges and stock markets, I always came back to the US. That's where I'd find the movers, the liquidity, the size. Not that I'm some whale of trading moving massive size for my own or managed accounts, but this is where you'll find the most volatility and the most candidates to actively play stocks. That's my take on it, and I invite anyone to try what feels right for them.

The most famous exchange is the NYSE, the New York Stock Exchange, with about 3,000 securities listed at the moment. Then there's NYSE Arca — Arca used to be Archipelago, an ECN we also covered in Module 1 — and it's currently the top US exchange for ETFs, trading more than 8,000 US-listed securities in general (probably including some Nasdaq and even NYSE stocks among that count), though Arca was originally and predominantly focused on ETFs.

For those who don't know, an ETF is somewhat like a mutual fund, except that unlike mutual funds — which still exist, still have billions and billions invested, but trade only once a day (you call your broker and give an order to buy or sell) — an ETF trades like a stock: you can get in and out at any time during the day by looking at its quote and trading it the way you want. ETFs hold several types of assets inside them — stocks, commodities, foreign exchange, all sorts of things under the regulation and denomination of exchange-traded funds — and cover different industries, sectors, asset classes, countries, and indices, from big caps to small caps to value stocks. You could also cover fixed income — US bonds, German bonds, French bonds, Italian bonds, European bonds, corporate bonds.

To my mind, ETFs have really revolutionized the way asset management is offered to people like ourselves — the "small guy" — because they enable a very diversified portfolio of liquid stocks that you can get in and out of, without paying the huge fees that real mutual funds charge. Even today, mutual funds can charge load fees of up to 5% just to get in — put in $1,000 and you pay $50 just to enter the fund — plus management fees that are also much higher than ETFs. So the creation of the ETF has been a real revolution for the small investor's perspective on asset management, giving access to a well-diversified portfolio. If you're not someone willing to take too much risk, or you're happy managing a portfolio for any purpose — aggressive growth, retirement income, etc. — you can set it up accordingly with ETFs, each of which holds hundreds if not thousands of different stocks, bonds, or currencies across whatever asset class and country you want to play in. We'll revisit this in later modules.

Then there's the Nasdaq, with about 2,300 securities listed at this stage, and the OTC market — the pink sheets — small companies that are semi-regulated or, in some cases, not regulated at all. On the NYSE and Nasdaq, companies have real obligations toward the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) — they must provide accounting on balance sheets, income statements, etc., and must meet strict rules to be listed. The OTC market, being over-the-counter, has much less strict regulation, and a lot of these companies are tiny — sometimes with no real business justifying their listing. We have to be careful trading these names, and careful about the credit risk we're undertaking.

## Advantages of Stock Market Trading Over Other Markets

Personally, I like that stock trading has regulated opening hours, as opposed to the over-the-counter market that's open 24/7. I like forex, but there was a time it would literally drive me crazy — when you've got a market that's almost always open (FX closes late Friday night, around 6 or 7pm New York time, and reopens Sunday night in Australia, giving you really just one day off), it can drive you nuts. If you're an impulsive trader, you could spend all your time clicking the mouse, which I really don't recommend — it puts a lot of pressure on you. Whereas with stock or futures markets that have regulated hours — in the US, the main session opens at 9:30am Eastern and closes at 4:00pm (there's pre-market and post-market trading too, but liquidity and volume don't really incline you to trade outside the main hours) — having regulated hours gives you a schedule you can actually follow. With 24/7 FX markets, you might wake up in the middle of the night to an alert telling you something's happening, and it can get a bit crazy — there was a time I'd be watching everything moving left, right, and center, and that's not the way to make money; it can drive you nuts.

Second point: being based in Europe (I'm currently in Greece, though I travel within Europe) gives me a perfect time zone. I can trade the US market when it opens at 4:30pm my time — if I dedicate 2-3 hours from the open, that's 4:30 to 7:30, though the session runs until 11pm my time, which is a bit late, but I can be in front of the TV checking quotes on my tablet or phone, or bring up my laptop if I see a stock moving, and I have my home office to monitor positions. The bulk of FX liquidity is still based in London, so London hours (7am to 5pm) is where you get the bulk of FX trading and liquidity — you also get the bulk of liquidity for some metals, definitely industrial metals, with the LME trading in London. So being based in Europe gives you the best of all worlds — on top of that, I trade my own accounts, other people's accounts, and I teach and coach traders. But even if you want to keep a regular 9-to-5 job, you can start trading the US stock market or other markets in your afternoon/evening, and being in Europe also gives us the tail end of Asian hours in the early morning, so we can dabble there too.

## Single Stocks vs. Indices: Volatility and Opportunity

I know traders who love trading the S&P 500 index, the Nasdaq index, the Russell 2000, or the DAX 30 (the most volatile European index). Some people are really good at it, and I've definitely tried it myself — I was originally one of the guys trading the S&P and the large indices. If you specialize and focus mostly on that, you can become very good and make a lot of money. But single stocks offer volatility you'll never find in indices — indices can get very rangy and choppy, evolving in a slow range for days until you finally get a signal worth trading. Single stocks, especially in the US, offer opportunities every single day — not just one opportunity, but many different opportunities every day.

Let's look at examples of the kind of "movers" I'm talking about, starting with pre-market top gainers and losers. On one day: a stock up 51%, another up 37%, one up 18% on decent volume, one down 35% but with no real volume (so not worth considering), and another down 24% in pre-market. Another day: a stock already up 75% pre-market on 1.5 million shares traded (about to open with a 75% gap), another up 36%, a penny stock at $0.33 with no volume (skip it), and one trading 240,000 shares pre-market already up 30%. On the loser side that same day: stocks opening down 9% and down 14% on real volume. Another day: a stock opening up 75% (a penny stock at $0.58), another trading heavily at 2.9 million shares pre-market (remember, half a million-plus shares pre-market already indicates a quite active pre-market session) about to open up 27%, another opening up over 20%, and on the downside, stocks opening down 34% and down 15%. Another day: a stock (a well-known 2017 name) up 90% on 700,000 shares, another up 32%, others down 8% and 20% (small volume on those last two).

That's pre-market. Now look at what happens after the session closes. On May 18th, top gainers were up 98%, 99%, 27%, and 35% — one particular stock went up almost 100% in a single session (9:30am to 4pm) on 37 million shares traded. Crazy, but that's what happens. Another day: stocks up 21%, 31%, and 319% on 12 million shares traded, plus others up 34% and 25% — all in one session. We saw similar examples back in the technical analysis module with all those charts — it's absolutely crazy, but in the US you get this kind of opportunity every single day, with real volume and liquidity behind it. On the loser side, on various days: stocks down 25%, -16%, -24%, and -24% again, with sizeable volume; and on another day, -20%, -30%, -45%, and -30%, with heavy volume of 6 million, 7 million, and 17 million shares.

Again, we're talking about a single daily session producing these kinds of top movers — and not just one stock, but many stocks. If you can only catch the "meat of the move" — part of a stock's 30%, 40%, or 300% move — that's more than enough. You'll never get that kind of volatility in an index, and you certainly won't get it in fixed income, buying corporate or junk bonds. The volatility and liquidity we're really looking for is mostly in single stocks.

## Why Volume Information Matters

What I also like about stock, futures, or index trading is that we get volume information, which is so important, as we've seen in previous technical analysis modules — a move validated by volume is far more credible. If the market drops big on unusually large volume, that's an important factor for our decision; the same goes for a big up move on big volume — we know there are real actors behind it, institutional players, and it's a genuine indication of real buying and selling power from major institutional and retail accounts, like myself and like you. This is valid information I like to work with, and you can't really do that with FX. There are platforms (shown in Module 1) that display some kind of "volume" for FX, but the reality is that the FX market is an OTC market where trillions of dollars trade daily from one dealer to another, and you never really see or know what's actually going through — there's no true indication of volume. Sometimes, especially with certain European currencies, a big overnight move can happen on essentially no meaningful volume, and you might think it's significant when it's not — but you can't tell in FX, whereas in stock trading, volume is a real, genuine piece of information we get. That's another thing I like about stock market trading.

## Revisiting Trend Concepts from Previous Modules

I want to revisit the concept of trends we covered in past modules — a quick revision to refresh our minds. In Module 2 we introduced trend trading through the Dow Theory, exponential moving averages, the volume-weighted average price (VWAP), and the volume-weighted moving average (VWMA) — the concept behind these moving averages is very similar, and we'll see more examples of how to apply them.

As mentioned, volume confirms the trend — that's part of the Dow Theory too: the more significant the volume behind any trading move or technical breakout, the more credible the move. We also talked about how a trend is assumed to be in effect until it gives definite signals that it has reversed. Let's revisit how we can detect a change or reversal of trend according to the Dow Theory.

First, the simple chart concept of resistance and support: blocks of price that act as resistance, with the market playing "ping-pong" between resistance and support until it breaks through to reach a new resistance level, then comes back down — with the previous resistance becoming the new support, and vice versa — until we reach a new resistance or break down through support. Basic stuff, but a useful reminder for what follows.

Remember also the failure swing and non-failure swing graph. In a failure swing, we look at higher highs and higher lows, until at some point this rule breaks — the market goes down and breaks a prior low (point B), establishing a lower low at a new point, which some traders would treat as a sell signal. It's called a "failure" swing because at the following peak, the market failed to go above the prior high, failing to establish a higher high. In a non-failure swing scenario, we do get higher highs and higher lows, then eventually break the equivalent of point B, establishing a lower low; we bounce back to that former support (now resistance), bounce off it, and break down again. Different schools of thought disagree on exactly where to call the trend reversal — some say at the first lower low, others insist on waiting for a second, confirmed break of the lower low before calling the reversal. Different schools, different traders — but an important reminder of the Dow Theory concept.

Finally, we revisited the EUR/USD example: establishing support around 1.18, bouncing back up, retracing but failing to exceed the prior high (a lower high), establishing a low, and bouncing back — at which point we could note a higher low compared to the previous one. Going up from there, we'd establish, per the Dow Theory, a change of trend — though some traders would want to wait for confirmation at a subsequent high rather than call it there. Different schools, but at that stage we definitely reach a higher high, giving us a higher low and a higher high. The market then retraces again, finds support, bounces back, and marks a series of higher lows and higher highs. That's the Dow Theory illustrated again on this EUR/USD example — a slide we've seen before, but worth the reminder.

## Horizontal Trend Lines and Price Convergence Areas

I personally strongly favor horizontal trend lines that act as support or resistance, as we saw in Module 2 when I showed you how I trace areas of convergence, the price clusters — most of the time I draw them as horizontal lines, which I think is the most proper way to do it. Some people prefer diagonal trend lines — a different school of thought — but I like horizontal lines because there's no leeway for interpretation: the low or high on a given day was the low and the high for everyone. Whereas with diagonal trend lines, you might draw through one set of lows or highs, while someone else draws through a different set, giving you a slightly different line. That's why I favor horizontal trend lines, and that's where most of my analysis happens — around price convergence areas or clusters.

To remind you again: price convergence areas and clusters are a very important part of our method for establishing areas of resistance and support that we watch closely for buying or selling decisions. We covered how to start constructing them at the end of Module 2, and we'll do more exercises during the live webinars where I'll show you again how to build them yourself. But it's part of your daily job — take whatever instruments you follow, draw your lines, your convergence areas, your price clusters, and establish the zones in which you'll take action, whether taking profit on a long or initiating a short. You have to have your price zones defined on your charts — this is important.

Also on the subject of support and resistance: remember what we said about round numbers — $5, $10, $50, $200, even $0.50 in cents-denominated names. These round numbers are important and often act as a first level of resistance and support. I'm not saying price stops at every single round number, but the 5s, 10s, 20s, 50s tend to act as a kind of mental, psychological level for a lot of traders — you'll see some people take profits at these levels or enter on a break of them. Keep that in mind.

## A Word on the "Self-Fulfilling Prophecy" of Technical Analysis

Something we haven't discussed yet, and I've always had a fairly balanced view on: the notion that technical analysis is a self-fulfilling prophecy. This has definitely been debated among different schools of thought, argued back and forth by many people. My take: as we saw in Module 1, technical analysis operates around three main sets of beliefs — if you don't really believe in those, you can't lend much credibility to technical analysis, and you'd be better off as a fundamental analyst, where you'd find more sense. But since so many people use technical analysis (in addition to fundamental analysis), you have a sheer number of people looking at the same things — moving averages, Fibonacci numbers, certain areas of resistance and support — and that creates a self-fulfilling element: because so many people believe you'll get resistance or support off the main moving averages, or off Fibonacci retracement levels, and the more people act on that belief (whether or not they understand why it works), the more that mass behavior tends to fulfill itself when we hit those levels. So, saying technical analysis is "only" a self-fulfilling prophecy — I don't fully buy that, because I believe technical analysis has real validity that we've already covered. But I also can't dismiss the fact that many people using it, and reacting to it, reinforces it. Both factors matter and should be taken into account.

## Training Your Eyes to Spot a Trend Quickly

The whole point of this chapter of the module is to train ourselves — to train our brains and our eyes — to be able to quickly and visually spot trends, whether you're swing trading or day trading, whether you're trading a fast-moving market live or doing your analysis when the market is closed. Your brain and eyes have to be trained to spot things quickly, because as I showed at the start of this module, there's a sheer number of stocks trading in the US alone, and if you count global stocks, we're talking hundreds of thousands. Obviously we have limited brain capacity, and we have to limit ourselves to a certain number of stocks — we'll cover in the next chapter how to decide which stocks, assets, or ETFs to follow for day trading or in a portfolio. But we have to train our brains to quickly spot the best opportunities according to our methodology and our needs, and to filter quickly. That's what we're going to do now.

Here's the checklist of things I look at on a chart:

1. **The EMAs (exponential moving averages) must be clearly trending up or down**, and the price has to be moving along above or below them. That's the number one thing I look at.
2. **The moving averages should be moving either in parallel, or clearly separated** (I'll show real examples of what I mean).
3. **The slope of the EMA.** Obviously, changing the chart scale (linear to logarithmic) or the screen size changes the apparent slope, and I agree — but in the general scheme of things, relative to where the price is evolving, the slope is something I look at.
4. **The EMAs should be stacked in the correct order.** In an uptrend, the shorter EMA (10) should be above the 20, above the 50, above the 200; in a downtrend, it's the reverse — 200, then 50, then 20, then 10. When I see this kind of stacking, I feel the odds of a good trade improve.
5. **The Dow Theory pattern of higher highs/higher lows or lower highs/lower lows**, depending on whether the market is trending up or down.
6. **The Donchian channels**, covered in Module 2 — important levels establishing resistance/support and areas where breakouts often occur. Visually, they appear as steps, like a vertical staircase going up or down, marking clear support/resistance levels; seeing that on the chart helps you quickly understand which side you should be on.
7. **Breakouts with volume**, which can validate a reversal or change of trend.

## Trainspotting: Real Chart Examples

Let's go through some real examples using TC2000 charting software.

**LOD (Basic Materials sector, weekly chart).** The 10, 20, 50, and 200 moving averages have been moving steadily down, with price evolving steadily below them — always below the 50, definitely below the 200 (which carries real weight even on a weekly chart, though the daily 200-day MA is the one most people watch). The moving averages have also been moving parallel to each other — another good sign of a well-established trend — with a nice, consistent downward slope, versus an earlier period where the slope was choppy and flat. There was also a long-term resistance/support level; once price broke through that main support, it signaled the market was willing to go a lot further down, still below all the moving averages — two of our criteria fulfilled right there (trending MAs, price below MAs, and parallel MAs with a nice slope). The EMAs were also stacked correctly — 10 below 20 below 50 below 200 — a sign of a well-established trend. The Dow Theory pattern of lower highs and lower lows was clearly visible throughout. The Donchian channels drew a clean downward staircase without being broken even once, reinforcing how strong the downtrend was. And we had a breakout with volume that gave a clear signal to go short, in line with the primary trend.

**DISH (Dish Network, daily chart).** The moving averages have clearly been turning down, with price mostly below them. When you get the 10 below the 20, below the 50, below the 200 — a clear downtrend, with price trading below all of them — that's a very strong downtrend. The moving averages were also parallel most of the way down, a good sign. The slope of the moving averages was quite steep during the main decline, flattening out during a choppier period in between, before a breakout with volume below a support level that had held for a while, followed by a bounce back against the moving averages and a resumption of the decline (with a steep, though not the steepest, slope). Lower highs and lower lows were established repeatedly (five clear instances of each), and the Donchian channels traced a regular downward staircase that wasn't broken until very recently.

**CIEN-type example.** This stock was in a nice uptrend, then broke below a major support level. Price started trending below the moving averages, which began moving steeply and in parallel, with price evolving below them. The Donchian channels broke down and started showing a downward staircase. Volume on the breakout day was only average, so we couldn't draw a strong conclusion from volume alone, but the way price broke down and behaved below the moving averages was telling. Visually, the trend was accepted as down, meaning we should be trading it on the downside. Could you try to play a corrective bounce here? Sure, and you could make money on it — but the main idea is to trade within the trend and wait for a corrective bounce back against the 50-day MA (which happened more than once) as the higher-probability trade. Lower highs and lower lows were established repeatedly — there was no reason to consider buying this market. A lot of the work ahead is on you: spending screen time scanning stocks and ETFs in downtrends and uptrends, applying these concepts, and training your eye to recognize the trend and which side of the market you should be trading. This has to become almost automatic — an automatism that comes with screen time and practice applying the methodology.

**GSAT (Global Star).** Another downtrend: price moved below the moving averages, including the important 50-day MA, entered a choppy consolidation area, then marked a new low breaking through the consolidation's support on considerably higher-than-usual volume, with the 10, 20, 50, and 200 MAs in convergence, all trending down. This isn't a market I'd even consider buying. Would I buy a corrective bounce here? Lower highs and lower lows were clearly established, with the MAs parallel to each other and in the correct order, price below them, and the Donchian channels showing the downward staircase pattern. **RDCM (Radcom communications).** This stock traded for a long time in a range roughly between $16.80/16.75 and $22.50, until a move down on massive volume broke below the main support that had held for a while — the stock dropped from about $17 all the way down to $11.50 in a single day, a huge move on huge volume (there was negative news that day). But was I looking to play the long side beforehand? No — the moving averages were already in convergence, with price comfortably below the 10, which was below the 20, below the 50, below the 200, and the MAs trending down in parallel with a nice slope. Would I have considered buying the bottom of the range beforehand? **TDM-type example (uptrend).** Exactly the same logic in reverse: moving averages showing a clear uptrend, moving parallel to each other, with price above them. **TCEHY/TCMDB-type example.** This stock traded in a range roughly between $23.27 and $38, with a pretty choppy market and flat moving averages throughout the range. The market then rose strongly, finding support on the 20-day MA a few times, then breaking down through it to find support around the 50-day MA (which lined up with prior price levels and Donchian levels) — an interesting area to buy the pullback, which is exactly what happened. On the weekly chart, since it's a relatively new stock there isn't much history, but you can see the same pattern: the big breakout, with the MAs separating rather than staying as parallel as in some previous examples, though the EMA slope is still quite steep compared to the earlier flat range — a period where there was nothing to do except perhaps play the range (sell the top, buy the bottom) if you wanted to, though there's no real money in that; it would just waste your time and equity compared to more attractive market situations.

**OPRT-type example.** Same type of configuration: a long range with absolutely flat moving averages, then a move up above the range on modest (not huge) volume — you won't always get the huge-volume breakout, sometimes the market just grinds slowly up or down. Here, price definitively cleared the main resistance and held above the moving averages, which began moving in parallel with a decent, if not overly steep, slope — not the most volatile stock, moving into long periods of lateral consolidation before advancing again. Was there ever a sense you'd want to short this stock? Not at all — higher highs, higher lows, a decent MA slope, the 10 above the 20, above the 50, above the 200, price comfortably above the MAs, with support found at the 20-day MA repeatedly, then at a second and third support level corresponding to a convergence of price with the Donchian levels and a former resistance turned support. Not the most exciting stock, but visually it should immediately tell you which side of the trade to take — certainly not short, no matter what.

**ABMD (healthcare sector, a strong sector this year).** Here we see big jumps where the moving averages separate quite a lot, with the 10 and 20 sticking close to price, which stayed above both. The market then consolidated, and price returned to test the 50-day MA as support multiple times (four times), until — for the first time — a prior low wasn't held, and we broke through to mark a new, lower low. That's the point where you should start being concerned about a potential change or reversal of trend. But up until that point, you shouldn't have entertained shorting this stock, aside from those who like to play corrective moves like the pullbacks seen along the way — there is money to be made on those, especially for intraday traders and scalpers, but that's not my style, and not what I'd advise; it ties up your equity on trades not worth taking. I'd much rather take the breakout — for instance here, on above-average volume — with the full convergence of signals: price breaking up on decent volume above the 10, 20, 50, and 200 MAs, nice convergence, nice slope, higher highs, higher lows, and the Donchian channels showing their staircase going up. That's the side of the trade I want to be on — no other side.

**A final weekly-chart example.** This stock consolidated in a range, had a fake breakout, went back into the range, and then finally had a big breakout. The moving averages were absolutely flat until the breakout on volume, after which the MAs separated from each other while showing a nice slope, with higher highs and higher lows — that's a market I want to be long, and certainly not short. ## The Most Important Rule: Trade With the Trend

We've just gone through quite a number of examples, and I showed you what I look at on a chart to make my decision. All of these elements give traders a quick, visual read on the current trend of a stock or any other financial instrument, and which side of the market we should be on.

But above all, let me remind you of the single most important rule: **do not trade against the main trend.** It's so important — the day you truly understand that, your trading is going to become very different, and much more profitable, than it was before. That means we should be looking to short in a downtrend, or buy in an uptrend, and not the other way around. Some people want to prove themselves, let their ego play out, and show the world that a market that's been falling has reached bottom, so they buy it — or vice versa, a market that's risen a lot must be due for a fall, so they short it. You might be right, you might be wrong — the important thing is not to let your ego dictate what you feel is right or wrong; let the market dictate that.

It's okay-ish to try to play a corrective move — going against the main trend, trying to pick a bottom or a top — but that has to be governed by very strict money and risk management rules; if you're not applying those rules very strictly, don't do it. I'm not saying the contrarian style is wrong — it's one legitimate style of trading, and I know traders and some very famous hedge fund managers who've been very successful at it. My own style, as I've said, is more of a trend follower, though that doesn't mean I'll never try a contrarian, corrective move in certain conditions — but I have to convince myself it's not an ego move, that I genuinely believe we're due for a correction within the bigger uptrend, downtrend, or main trend, and I have to apply very strict rules around my money and risk management: I know where I'm getting out, and if I'm wrong, I get out, period. Because so many people try to be heroes, letting their ego dictate wanting to be the first to buy the low or sell the high and show the world "look what I did" — and then, if they're wrong, letting their ego dictate that they shouldn't get out, that they should let it play out because they'll probably be right eventually. That's when you burn your whole account, because you didn't respect your own risk and money management.
