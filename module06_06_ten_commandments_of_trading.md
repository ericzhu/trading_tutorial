# Module 6, Video 6: The Ten Commandments of Trading Academy

This final video of Module 6 covers the "Ten Commandments of Trading Academy" — the essential rules and guidelines that tie together the three core strategies and the multiple time frame (MTF) convergence system into a single practical checklist to run through before every trade, much like a pilot's pre-flight checklist. The more this checklist is used, the more familiar the method and guidelines become. It's recommended to print these Ten Commandments and review them daily until they become second nature.

## Commandment 1: Be Prepared
Keep strict discipline in doing pre-market analysis and building a trading plan — there is no excuse for being unprepared. Draw support/resistance lines, Donchian channels, EMAs, and Fibonacci levels on the monthly, weekly, daily, and 4-hour timeframes, and identify all the price support/resistance convergence areas. The majority of trades should be planned before the market opens, not decided in the heat of the moment.

In practice, this means the stocks/candidates reviewed the night before must be planned trades — ones you've already thought through as potential setups. The goal every morning before the US market opens is to have **2–3 candidates at most**, plus perhaps a list of 2–3 additional stocks already identified from prior planning, all ready to act on with their technical levels and breakout levels already worked out. New candidates will also pop up during the day from scans — these need to be analyzed quickly, and with practice this becomes easier over time. Preparing trades the night before, while the market is closed, helps collect your thoughts and make decisions without rushing, and trains your eyes/brain to recognize opportunities quickly when live market action unfolds.

## Commandment 2: Never Trade Against the Principal Trend
At minimum, price needs to have crossed the daily EMA 20 against the recently established principal trend before even considering a potential trend change, per the core strategy rules covered earlier. Remember the overall approach: the strategic direction is decided on the longer timeframe (monthly, weekly, daily), tactical moves are executed on the shorter timeframe (4-hour, 1-hour, 15-minute), weekly MACD divergence should be monitored (especially with a double top/bottom), and price needs to be above the relevant EMA to go long (and vice versa for short) — this applies across all core strategies.

## Commandment 3: Focus on the Big Movers
Use scanners (covered in the next module) to find candidates, and keep tracking the best movers over the following 1–2 weeks. Sometimes a scanner candidate doesn't do anything the day it's flagged (or even the next day), but 2–3 days later the right chart configuration and setup can appear — this is what patience and a watch list are for. Keep 5–10 stocks researched over the weekend/week on the back burner and revisit them during the day when conditions match your parameters and setups.

Also ignore non-volatile, boring stocks. The whole style is about finding where the action/momentum is — the mover of the day or week — to maximize opportunity and return on capital. Avoid stocks stuck in a tight, non-moving range; instead look at the state and steepness of moving averages and other scanning parameters to identify genuine potential movers.

Look for solid news catalysts accompanied by technical breakout or breakdown moves. Don't buy a stock just because it's "on discount" after being down 50–70% in a day — if you do, you need a very compelling reason, a predefined stop loss and target profit, and the recognition that going contrarian against a big move means going against the crowd.

## Commandment 4: Do Not Give In to FOMO
Don't force trades — be patient and wait for the right entry point, letting the best opportunity come to you. Good opportunities typically look like a bounce/pullback — a high-probability value trade, ideally when price returns to an EMA, or even better, when resistance/support/Fibonacci levels converge at the same price and time.

Don't trade on momentum bias just because you feel you missed the move — that's not a good enough reason. If there's no pullback, move on and wait for the next opportunity; missing a trade is perfectly fine, since the market will still be there tomorrow and the day after.

Make sure your entry price isn't too close to major support/resistance areas — check moving averages, round numbers, Donchian levels, recent highs/lows, etc., since entry levels directly determine the risk/reward ratio you'll get (always aiming for a minimum risk/reward ratio of 1 for TP1).

A good trade is the combination of a good entry price, good timing, excellent position management, and — most importantly — good exits, correct sizing, and tight risk management. Above all, stick to your trading plan; that discipline is the only way to succeed in trading.

## Commandment 5: Leave Enough Room for the Trade to Develop
As covered in Module 5, stops must be placed at real invalidation points. It's wiser to use a larger stop loss with a smaller position size, especially when trying to catch a major turning point or reversal. Once a trend is established, tighter stops using shorter timeframes become more workable for intraday trading.

If stopped out at a profit or breakeven but the trade strategy is still valid, look for a re-entry point. If stopped out at a loss, that's fine too — proper risk management makes losses acceptable and manageable. Remember that every single trade is nothing more than a probability of outcome, nothing else.

## Commandment 6: Don't Wait to Be Stopped Out if the Trade Isn't Behaving as Intended
It's often better to bail out of half the position — for example, when the market reintegrates a range or breakout area — and look to re-enter later if the setup is still valid. This requires fully divorcing yourself from your emotions, admitting when you're wrong and the trade isn't performing as planned. Being profitable matters more than being right — leave your ego at home. Capital preservation is the key, the single most important thing.

## Commandment 7: Take Partial Profits at Pre-Established Targets
Move the stop loss to breakeven as soon as TP1 is achieved, always aiming for a minimum risk/reward ratio of 1 on TP1. If the market keeps moving in your favor after TP1, stay with the trend and maximize profits by waiting for further take-profit targets.

The best strategies, especially in day trading, aim to hit many signals and consistently bank modest profits (like TP1) — these accumulate over time into a solid "war chest." Don't try to hit a home run on every single trade. Also, don't be greedy: if a limit order to open or close a position isn't quite filled but comes very close, revisit the trade and consider entering/exiting at a slightly worse level if the trade's logic is still intact.

Aim to capture the "meat" of the move rather than trying to buy the exact bottom or sell the exact top — the core strategies are inherently difficult because they aim to catch inflection points/major turns in the trend, which is never an easy thing to do, which is exactly why strict rules exist for how these strategies are implemented. Often you won't catch the whole move, and that's not the goal. Capturing the middle of the move, even if some trades are only stopped out at breakeven after TP1, still banks real money, builds consistency, and grows your account equity — even slowly. You only need one outsized trade (5–6× your initial risk) once a week or even once every two weeks to really put you ahead.

## Commandment 8: Focus on About Five Trading Instruments
When starting out, even three is more than enough. When starting intraday trading, use one position (two at most) at least in the beginning. Do not combine highly correlated positions — such as similar positions in different FX pairs like GBP pairs, or correlated indices like Nasdaq and S&P.

Do not use leverage, or if you must, use it within reason (as covered in Module 5). Make sure your position size/exposure is psychologically acceptable and tolerable to you — if it's not in sync with your psychology or nature as a trader, you'll feel under pressure, and that's when the biggest mistakes happen.

## Commandment 9: Don't Compare Yourself to Others
This isn't strictly a "commandment," but a reminder not to compete with anyone else to make more money. It's pointless to compare yourself to person X, Y, or Z — your main concern should be making enough money to support your family, cover your living expenses, or generate the extra income you're aiming for. Remember that just one good, high-probability trade once or twice a week is often all it takes to make a decent living and free yourself from a day job.

## Commandment 10: Listen Only to What the Market Is Telling You Now
This final commandment draws again from Marty Schwartz's book, *Pit Bull: Lessons from Wall Street's Champion Day Trader*: "You can listen only to what the market is telling you now. Forget about what you thought it was telling you five minutes ago." The main objective of trading isn't to prove you're right, but to hear the cash register ring. Most people think they're playing against the market, but the market doesn't care — you're really playing against yourself. It's a mind game, mostly psychological.

Stop trying to will things to happen just to prove you're right. Trading isn't easy, but never give up — success is always around the corner if you persevere with the same strategy and keep growing every day as a trader. In times of stress and drawdown, simply reduce trade size (as covered in Module 5) — this makes the drawdown more tolerable and helps rebuild confidence, allowing you to gradually work back up in size from small, "fresh start" trades.

Also very important: keep a trading journal and study it constantly to understand and concentrate on your quality setups and your recurring mistakes. Only by writing down and reviewing every single trade — at the end of each day and each week — will you understand what you're good at, what you're bad at, and be able to refine and clean up your trading. This will be covered further in the next module, Module 7.

## Closing Note
These are the Ten Commandments. Print them, keep them next to your trading desk, and review them at least once every day. Over time they will penetrate your mind, body, and spirit until you're fully in sync — an internal alarm bell will start going off, telling you "I shouldn't be doing this" or "I should be doing that." Keep reading these Ten Commandments, keep your checklist close at hand, and everything will be all right.
