## Fibonacci Retracement

As explained in the PDF, Fibonacci numbers matter because of the so-called golden ratio, 1.618 — in the Fibonacci sequence, any given number is approximately 1.618 times the preceding number. This golden ratio has shown up across architecture, nature, music, design, and painting for thousands of years, and more and more traders believe it also plays a role in price patterns. Whether this is partly a self-fulfilling belief — similar to how moving averages work simply because enough traders watch them — I won't dive too deeply into why it works. The fact remains that the golden ratio has been observed throughout human history in many contexts, and it may apply to trading as well.

**How to draw it:** identify the most recent significant high and the most recent significant low, then draw the Fibonacci retracement between them. Any charting software (TC2000 or otherwise) has this tool built in. The key retracement levels are 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, and 100%.

**Example 1 (weekly chart):** after a sharp two-day rally, the high of the move stopped almost exactly at the 61.8% retracement level. I've personally observed — and many other traders have too — that the 50%–61.8% retracement zone is where price very often reacts. I personally focus on three levels: 38.2%, 50%, and 61.8%. I use these levels to add to my cluster of support/resistance levels when building areas of price convergence (covered later). Once price enters that 50%–61.8% zone, that's when you want to watch closely and consider a decision — take profits, or watch for a reaction.

**Example 2 (CAT):** drawing from the recent high down to the recent low, price first reacted around the 38.2% level (my preferred zone), then in a bigger move, price stalled almost exactly on the $12 round number, which also happened to be the 61.8% retracement. On the daily chart, that day showed a huge volume spike (6.4 million vs. a 326,000 average — nearly 20x), with price stopping right at $12/61.8%, followed by a retracement the next day.

**Example 3 (Facebook, weekly, 2016–2018):** drawing the Fibonacci from the 2016 low to the February 2018 high, when the stock started correcting, the 50%–61.8% zone lined up with an area of established prior price support (where the stock had spent many weeks consolidating and accumulating). This convergence of Fibonacci support and prior price support is exactly the kind of setup to watch for a potential long entry, even though price is below the moving averages at that point.

**Example 4 (Twitter, weekly):** using the low and high of the past two years, the preferred 50%–61.8% zone coincided with a convergence of the 50-day and 200-day moving averages, and price reacted immediately upon reaching it. After a bounce to resistance (the 10/20-week moving averages) and a break of the recent low near $31, price found support at the 61.8% level — which also lined up with a double bottom and the 26-week/52-week Donchian levels (6-month/12-month lows). Even though the broader trend is down and caution is warranted, this is not an area to be aggressively short — instead, watch for signs of a bounce.

**Example 5 (daily chart):** drawing the Fibonacci from a recent low to a recent high during a clear breakdown, the 50%–61.8% zone lined up with price consolidating around the 50-day moving average near $65. Even in a downtrend, this is an area to either wait for a further breakdown or watch carefully for a bounce play — which is what eventually happened, breaking out on huge volume up through $9.44, stalling near $10.23 (a prior high), and failing to reach the older high at $12.64 before a successful downside breakout (as covered in the breakouts video). Multiple consolidation areas here lined up with the 50-day moving average and the 50%–61.8% Fibonacci zone.

## ATR (Average True Range)

The ATR is a tool I use to gauge daily volatility for a stock or any financial instrument. The commonly used ATR takes a 14-day moving average of the **true range** — the true range being the maximum range of price movement between the prior close and the current period (you can look up the exact formula easily). Most charting software plots a 14-day average true range automatically.

I also like to look at the **1-day ATR** (the true range for just that single day), often paired with a **Bollinger Band** around it. Bollinger Bands are based on statistical inference — typically two standard deviations around a 20-day simple moving average — giving a sense of the expected volatility range.

**How I use it:** on any given day, if the 1-day ATR reaches the top of its Bollinger Band (i.e., a two-standard-deviation move), that tells me we've likely already seen the bulk of that day's move, so I should be cautious about chasing further. Naturally, on days with a huge breakdown or breakout (like the Twitter example, where the 1-day true range spiked to $3 versus recent history), this gauge blows out — that's expected on days with massive volume and a big green/red candle, and the concept of "full move already happened" doesn't apply the same way. But on an average day with decent (not extraordinary) volume, checking the 1-day ATR against its Bollinger Band helps gauge whether most of the day's move has already occurred, encouraging more prudent decisions.

I also sometimes use ATR in stop-loss calculations — e.g., placing a stop at two or two-and-a-half times the ATR below entry. Because ATR is a standardized volatility gauge, it works consistently across instruments: low-volatility instruments (bonds, utility stocks) will have a small ATR, while high-volatility instruments (tech stocks, cannabis stocks) will have a large ATR. Using ATR-based stops means your stop placement automatically adapts to the underlying instrument's volatility.

## MACD Divergence

I won't cover MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) in full detail since it's in the PDF. It's a very popular oscillator, as famous as stochastics or RSI. I personally used to run MACD, RSI, stochastics, and a whole battery of indicators on my charts — but over years of experience I've eliminated most of them, because at the end of the day you want to keep your methodology simple enough to explain on a napkin, understandable even to a five-year-old. Indicators like these are lagging, since they're derived from price itself — starting analysis from raw price action instead keeps you faster than traders who follow a battery of derived indicators.

That said, MACD remains useful, and I've narrowed my use of it specifically to **divergence** — when price and the MACD indicator disagree about the trend.

**Example 1 (EUR/USD):** price keeps making new lows, but MACD fails to make a corresponding new low (it actually bounces) — a divergence, a red flag that price action isn't confirmed by the indicator, suggesting caution on a short position (e.g., covering part of it). Sure enough, the market reversed and rallied to a new high.

**Example 2 (DAX index, weekly):** price makes a new high above 13,000, but MACD doesn't confirm — first warning sign. Later, price consolidates back near the old high (~13,500) while MACD continues declining — a strong divergence building over many months (starting around June 2017). Combined with a double top (an earlier warning) and this weekly MACD divergence (a second warning), the market eventually breaks down through major support and the 50-day moving average, with MACD turning firmly negative.

**Example 3 (CAD/JPY, weekly) — a full divergence walkthrough:** price is in a steady downtrend below its 200- and 50-day moving averages, ranging (with a near-triple top around 75.41) between July and November. Meanwhile, MACD shows higher lows (a bullish divergence) even as price makes double/triple bottoms — a warning that the downtrend may be losing steam. Sure enough, price rallies strongly to ~87.30, with MACD turning firmly green.

At the next resistance (the 200-day moving average) and a subsequent double top, the MACD average confirms the new high, but the histogram does not — a partial/mixed confirmation, worth noting since ideally both the MACD lines and the histogram should agree. Later, at a further double top, both the MACD lines *and* the histogram show lower highs — a full divergence and a genuine warning sign, compounded by a near-complete engulfing pattern at that same double top. This combination (double top + divergence + engulfing pattern at a weekly resistance level) is a strong signal, and indeed price subsequently breaks down hard, with MACD crossing over and the histogram turning deeply red as price breaks through major support and moving averages.

### Rules for Trading MACD Divergence

Because MACD divergence is easy to over-apply (seeing it "everywhere" on lower timeframes like hourly or 15-minute charts dilutes its reliability), I stick to three strict conditions:

1. **Timeframe**: look at divergence on at least the daily chart — I personally prefer the weekly, where divergences tend to mark the most significant turning points.
2. **Pattern spacing**: look for divergence alongside a double top/bottom formation, with the two tops/bottoms not spaced more than roughly 2–6 months apart (no hard rule, but beyond ~6 months the signal loses reliability).
3. **Histogram confirmation**: the MACD histogram should fail to reach back to its previous high/low from the first top/bottom — i.e., the histogram itself shows the divergence, not just the MACD lines.

When all three conditions align (as in the CAD/JPY example), my rule is simple: if long and this signal appears at a double top with a strong MACD divergence, automatically close 50% of the position — no debate, especially after a confirming engulfing candle. Depending on subsequent price action, I might close the rest of the position and look for a short setup. The finer details of execution come later, once we cover the three core strategies.

## Building Areas of Price Convergence

The last topic: drawing resistance and support zones. This sounds simple — and largely is — but requires training your eye. Support and resistance shouldn't be treated as a single specific price; instead, the goal is to build "cluster zones" where multiple signals converge, indicating areas of interest for entering or exiting a position. The same process applies to any instrument — forex, stocks, or commodities.

**Step-by-step process (demonstrated on an FX pair):**

1. **Monthly chart**: identify significant highs and lows (helped visually by where the Donchian channels are longest) and draw horizontal lines at those levels. The higher the timeframe under consideration, the more significant the resulting support/resistance level. (Note: TC2000 isn't ideal for FX — platforms like TradingView or ProRealTime offer much more historical FX data; TC2000 is used here just for the exercise, being better suited to US stocks.)
2. **Weekly chart**: repeat the process, again guided by the Donchian channels (here using the 26-week/52-week channels, marking 6-month and 12-month highs/lows) to flag additional significant highs/lows.
3. **Daily chart**: repeat again, focusing on highs/lows close to current price rather than the whole chart history, again cross-checking against the Donchian channels.
4. **4-hour chart**: repeat once more. (For FX, most of my swing trades are built around signals from the daily and 4-hour timeframes — a personal preference; a shorter-term trader might use lower timeframes, while a longer-term trader might stick to weekly/monthly, though that yields far fewer signals. Daily + 4-hour tends to produce trades that hold for days to a few weeks.)
5. **1-hour chart**: repeat again for any additional relevant highs/lows.
6. **15-minute chart**: repeat one final time.

At this point the chart looks fairly cluttered with horizontal lines — that's expected. Zooming in, the areas where many of these horizontal lines cluster together are exactly the "cluster zones" — the more lines converging in one area, the stronger that zone will act as resistance or support.

**Adding moving averages:** since price is currently trending up and just pulled back through the 10/20-period moving averages toward the 50, draw a small band around the current 50-day moving average level (expected support) and another around the 200-day moving average.

**Adding Fibonacci retracement:** taking the most recent low-to-high move (the point where the market last turned on the 4-hour chart) and drawing the Fibonacci retracement shows the 38.2% level landing right in the middle of one of these cluster zones — reinforcing it further.

**Putting it together:** with the broader trend up, having recently broken through a major resistance level and now consolidating, the highest-probability trade is to look for a long entry. The 4-hour chart's nearest, most densely clustered support zone — where the horizontal cluster lines, the 50-day moving average, and the 38.2% Fibonacci level all converge — becomes the first area to watch closely for a potential bounce, whether adding to an existing long or initiating a new one.

To be clear, this exercise isn't about predicting what the market *will* do — it's about being prepared. These cluster zones are where we expect the market either to find support and bounce back into its prevailing uptrend, or to break through and signal something different. Either way, having these zones mapped out in advance means being ready to act on whatever the market decides. The precise entry mechanics, and how this fits into the three core strategies, will be covered in a later chapter.
