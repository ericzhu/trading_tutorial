# Module 7, Video 2: Preparing Your Weekly List of Candidates (Weekend Routine)

This video continues the theme of preparing for the trading session, walking through the exact weekend process used to build the weekly list of candidates.

## Why Prepare During the Weekend

The best time to do this preparation work is while most markets are closed (crypto being the exception, trading 24/7). With no blinking quotes, no urge to click the mouse, no pressure from a losing position, and no greed from a winning one, this quiet time allows for a cold, impartial analysis. Trading plans can be built away from the chaos of live markets — all that's left to do is show up when the market opens (Sunday night for some markets, Monday morning for others) and follow the plan, whatever happens.

## Step 1: Open the Charting Software and the Full Watch List

1. Open the charting software (TC2000 in this case — any charting software the trader is comfortable with works).
2. Open the "US Composite Full" watch list — a list built within TC2000 consisting of just under **27,000 securities** at the time of recording. It includes stocks from the Nasdaq, bulletin boards, Pink Sheets, NYSE Euronext, the Amex, and other exchanges. **No ETFs are included in this list on purpose** — ETFs are kept as a separate process (covered later in this video).

## Step 2: Top-Down Filtering of the Full List

With 27,000 securities to filter, a top-down selection approach is used.

### 2a. Year-to-Date Ranking
- Rank all stocks by **year-to-date percentage performance**, starting from the top.
- Rationale: an object in motion stays in motion until a contrary force intervenes — a year-to-date high performer is on its way up and outperforming thousands of peers, which clearly indicates strong institutional presence. Institutions typically invest for the medium-to-long run, and when they deploy capital into a name, it takes time to complete the transaction — leaving a visible trace in the price action.
- Apply two additional filters:
  - **Average daily volume** of at least **1 million shares/day** (based on the past 20 days).
  - **Minimum market capitalization** of **$500 million**.
- Retain the **top 100 stocks** (top 100 year-to-date performers) and copy them into a new watch list called **"Top 100 Stocks."**

### 2b. Weekly Percentage Ranking
- Run a second ranking on the same list using the same volume and market-cap filters, but this time ranking by **weekly percentage change** — this keeps the list current with the most recent movers.
- Retain the **top 100 weekly performers** and copy them into the same "Top 100 Stocks" watch list.
- The end result is usually a combined list of about **160–180 stocks**, meaning there's a common overlap of roughly **20–30 outstanding stocks** that appear in both the year-to-date and weekly rankings.

## Step 3: Run Pullback Scanners on the Top 100 Stock List

With the ~180-name list built, the next step is to identify the best opportunities by applying the **multi-time-frame (MTF) conversion system**: trading within the trend established on Screen #1 and looking for a counter-trend entry on Screen #2.

- Scanners are programmed to run through the Top 100 Stock list and visually flag stocks with the best trends — ones about to pull back to crucial support levels, or about to break out.
- Personal style leans toward trend-following, buying pullbacks near value/EMA levels — but the choice is ultimately up to each trader's own personality.
- Process: go through the scanned candidates, isolate the best setups, mark intervention levels (price alerts or condition alerts such as the crossing of a moving average), and take notes — all to be ready to act when the market opens.
- The most favorite candidates are copied into what's called the **"A list"** (also referred to as the **"favorites"** list), which is strictly followed each coming day.
- This A list is also exported to **Trade Ideas**, a more advanced scanner than TC2000, especially for intraday signals and reaction speed. (Note: these scanners are made available to course members upon request.)

## Step 4: Look for Underdogs About to Reverse

Attention now turns to the "underdogs" — stocks that have fallen out of favor and been smashed down, the exact opposite of the momentum stocks sought earlier.

- The process is reversed: rank the **bottom 100 year-to-date** performers and the **bottom 100 weekly** performers, using the same volume/market-cap filters.
- The resulting list (roughly 170–200 stocks) is placed into a watch list called **"Bounce Reversal Long Plays."**
- The goal is to find the hidden gem — a downtrodden stock that may have reached a reversal point. This draws on the **Major Reversal Warning (MRA)** strategy covered earlier:
  - Major monthly or weekly area of support or resistance.
  - MACD divergence.
  - Signs of a double bottom forming.
  - A challenge to Dow Theory's pattern of lower lows.
- A set of scanners is used to identify potential candidates within this Bounce Reversal Long Plays list.

## Step 5: Short Candidates

The same reversed approach can be used to find stocks likely to keep falling for short candidates — scanning for pullbacks or breakouts to the downside on the bottom 100 (year-to-date and weekly) stock list.

Personal notes on shorting:
- Not a big fan of shorting single stocks personally, though many traders have found their niche and do very well with short setups — it's a matter of personal fit, not a style to copy carbon-for-carbon. Successful traders focus on their own trading, not on others (though it's fine to borrow ideas from many traders and adapt them to one's own needs).
- When shorting, ETFs are generally preferred over single stocks because single-name credit risk is diluted across many companies. Single-stock shorts can be faster and more profitable, but the risks are also considerably higher.
- Put options are occasionally bought for high-conviction short ideas on a particular name (covered in Module 9, dedicated to options trading).
- As a rule, shorting is avoided on stocks trading under the **$9–$10** mark, since these are considered to offer limited profit potential relative to the risk involved — though some traders do successfully short penny stocks. Again, it comes down to personal fit.

## Step 6: Recent Breakouts

Returning to the initial US Composite Full watch list (~27,000 stocks), scanners are used to identify recent breakouts above the **1-month, 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month highs**.

- These scanners are programmed around specific trend-convergence filters as well as the price-level breakout itself.
- To cut down the resulting list (which can run 150–200 names), filter again by minimum average daily volume, volume traded, and top percentage change over the last day.
- This typically narrows things down to 5–10 preferred names based on price setup, of which roughly half end up retained in the favorites/A list.

## Weekend Summary: The Target Outcome

The weekend analysis should result in a list of a **maximum of 10–15 strong names** that feel comfortable and appealing. For every single name on that list, a **clear trading plan** must be established for how to play it from Monday morning's market open through the rest of the week. This list should provide plenty of opportunities to apply the various strategies covered throughout the course.

## ETF Analysis (For the Active Investor)

The final part of the weekend routine turns to the ETF landscape — geared more toward the "active investor" style discussed earlier in this module (versus the strict day trader), used for **active portfolio investing** with a longer-term (medium-to-long) view, such as for a self-managed pension or other longer-term portfolios. Because this is not day trading, the multiple time-frame windows used are longer — **weeklies and dailies** are typically the minimum time frames considered.

### A Different Selection Approach for ETFs

Unlike stocks — where selection starts from the huge universe of the US Composite Full list and works down through filters — ETF selection instead starts with a **core list** representing a well-diversified portfolio of assets. This core list, called **"My ETF,"** may evolve over time depending on the liquidity of its components, but is generally quite stable.

- At the time of recording, "My ETF" consists of **24 constituents**, spanning the fixed income, equity, and commodity sectors, with further layers within each. It is not an exhaustive list, but covers most of the major asset-class components relevant to a medium-to-long-term active investment portfolio.
- Generic market indices are included — such as **SPY** (S&P 500) and **IWM** (Russell 2000) — to track the general trend and direction of the market, but the real focus is on concentrating on specific parts of the market.

### Sector Focus: The 11 S&P Sectors

The main focus is on the **11 main S&P sectors**, in order to invest in the most-performing sector of the general market — the one showing relative strength compared to the broader market. A similar top-down analysis to the one used for stocks is applied here:

- A separate watch list, **"Spider/Shares Fidelity ETF Sectors,"** is built in TC2000, including most of the main sector ETFs available.
- Because this list is also fairly large, it's filtered by average daily volume, then ranked by year-to-date and weekly percentage performance — the same process as with stocks.
- Note that this produces a lot of duplicate sectors, since the list pulls sector ETFs from multiple ETF providers. The important part is ranking these sectors the same way as with stock selection, concentrating on the **top 3–4 sectors** of the list.

### Bringing It Together

Once the top 3–4 specific sectors are identified in addition to the core "My ETF" list, the usual MTF analysis and scanners are run to identify pullback and breakout opportunities on the top-ranked ETFs — the best candidates on the list. The same underdog-watching process used for stocks is also applied here.

### The Target Portfolio

Ultimately, the goal is to build a medium-to-long-term portfolio made up of a **maximum of 10 to 15 ETF lines**, while keeping in mind the risk management rules from Module 5: risking **0–3% per trade**, with position size capped at a **maximum nominal exposure of 10% of the portfolio's size**.
