# Module 5, Video 4: Risk Management, the Danger of Leverage, and the R-Multiple Concept

Now that we've looked at money management in detail — one of its crucial aspects being position sizing — we need to tackle the other key aspect of our investing process: **risk management**. In the last few slides, we already introduced this concept by setting ourselves instructions on how to size our positions, including imposing a maximum risk per trade, or X percent of our equity.

The single factor of mitigating our risk and planning for the worst — preparing ourselves in case we're wrong in our assessment of the market and our trade — is what's called risk management. A more precise definition: in the financial world, risk management is the process of identification, analysis, and acceptance or mitigation of uncertainty in investment decisions. Basically, we attempt to quantify the potential for losses in an investment and plan the appropriate course of action to contain those potential losses, staying on course to achieve our investment objectives within our risk tolerance.

In a nutshell, we are really trying to prepare ourselves — trying to plan for the worst and make sure we'll act on it appropriately, so we can stay in the game and keep playing, because losses will happen. We know we might even go through consecutive series of losses — we know that. We need to believe in our system: if our system's expectancy shows us, over medium-to-long-term testing, that we should be making money, then we should be making money. There's no reason we shouldn't — but we need to be able to plan for that and stay in the game.

Money management and risk management work hand in hand — they complete each other, almost like Han Solo and Chewie. You can't have one without the other. Understanding and measuring risk enables us to choose one investment or trade over another, because we can assess our potential gain versus our potential loss.

## Risk-Reward Ratio

We touched on this concept when we discussed the payoff ratio — comparing average winning trades to average losing trades. We already said this payoff ratio needs to be at a minimum of 1 to 1: for every $1 I win on average, I lose $1. Not great, but the very minimum we want in our system — the higher, the better, as we saw clearly in the risk-of-ruin tables.

So when considering a new trade, we need to assess whether we're rewarded enough relative to the risk we're taking on. This risk versus reward is called the **RR** (risk-reward ratio), and it's directly linked to our payoff factor. This RR ratio also needs to be, at the very minimum, equal to or above 1 — ideally 2 or 3.

## Understanding Leverage

Another very important aspect of risk management is understanding the effect of leverage. You've heard me mention this "dirty word" many times throughout this training, and I've warned you about its dangers repeatedly — but now that we're dealing fully with the subject of risk, let me open this parenthesis properly.

**What is leverage?** It's the ability our financial intermediaries — brokers, banks, securities houses — give us, their customers, to play bigger than we should. With a small sum of money, we can command a much, much bigger sum.

**Example:** Say I have a $10,000 account at a broker where I can trade FX with 2% margin — meaning that margin is the minimum amount required to hold a position. With $2,000 of margin, I could command a trade size of up to $100,000 worth of Canadian dollar. In other words, I'm 50 times leveraged. Yes — with $2,000, I can buy the equivalent of $100,000.

Sounds great, doesn't it? If I made just 2% on the trade, that's a $2,000 profit, going straight into my account — a 20% return on my $10K account. Fantastic! Nothing to complain about — but because I'm a cautious guy who likes to assess the worst-case scenario first, let's look at the not-so-rosy side: what happens if the trade doesn't go our way.

**Trader One vs. Trader Two:** Trader one is leveraged 50 times, Trader two is leveraged only 10 times. In the case of a 50-pip losing trade (50 pips in FX means absolutely nothing — it can happen in a minute, especially around an economic data release):

- **Trader one** would be down a whopping **25%** on equity.
- **Trader two**, five times less leveraged, is only down **5%**.

It doesn't take a math genius to figure out which trader goes bust first, and very quickly — especially given our earlier discussion of the random distribution of losing trades and the possibility of a long, continuous string of consecutive losses. Trader one goes bust.

### Real Nominal Exposure

You absolutely need to check your real nominal exposure at all times, compared to your net equity. What do I mean by "real nominal exposure"? In the case of Trader one, the transaction value is $500,000 — $10,000 of real equity, but $500,000 of real nominal exposure: 50 times exposed. Trader two has a real nominal exposure of $100,000 against a $10,000 deposit: 10 times leverage.

You have to compare your real nominal exposure to your net equity every single time to calculate your leverage. With one position, that's easy. With multiple positions, you need to add up the real nominal exposure of all your current positions and divide that total by your current equity to arrive at your leverage ratio.

For example, if a broker gave you 100x leverage and you wanted two positions of $500,000 each (like Trader one), you'd have $1,000,000 of total exposure against a $10,000 account — even across just two positions, $500,000 + $500,000 = $1 million total real nominal exposure, compared to only $10,000 of equity.

### Leverage Guidelines by Experience Level

- **Beginners:** I sincerely advise **no leverage at all** to start. Trade only with the money you have in your account — no risk beyond that. Play with the market, understand your methodology, start getting used to it, start understanding your stop loss and the market you're trading.
- **Intermediate traders:** Once you're used to it, understand it a bit more, and are consistent and confident, you can go up to about a **2x leverage factor** — so with a $10,000 account, up to maybe 2x-3x, i.e., $20,000-30,000 of real nominal exposure against $10,000 equity.
- **Advanced traders:** Stay below a **3x-5x leverage** factor — so with a $10,000 account, real nominal exposure between $30,000-50,000. These are traders who understand the methodology, have become consistent, and can emotionally digest trading a leveraged account to some extent.

## The Stop Loss and the R-Multiple Concept

Now that we all understand the devastating impact too much leverage can have on an account — even to the point of near destruction — we can look in more detail at how to protect our trades effectively through the single most effective tool in the risk management arsenal: the **stop loss order**. Before covering that in detail, I'd like to introduce a useful related concept: the **R multiple**.

In his famous book *Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom*, Van Tharp (whom we quoted earlier) addresses the concept of R multiples — though I'm not sure he was the first to introduce it, I'll give him credit for using it in his approach to money management and risk management.

As discussed, the payoff ratio represents the relative size of your profits compared to your losses. A more useful measure is to think of gains as multiples of the **initial risk**, which we'll call **R**, taken when we entered the trade. When we decide to take a trade, we set ourselves an initial risk we're willing to lose — say $500 or $1,000. This number usually comes from a fixed percentage of equity we set for ourselves from the start — 1%, 1.5%, 2%, whatever feels comfortable to you. Let's say this 1%-of-equity risk becomes our initial risk — we define that as **R**.

When we measure our gains, they can be seen as a whole series of R multiples. For example, say we're willing to risk $500 on a trade — so our basic risk/stop loss is $500. In this case:
- A **$1,000 gain** = a **2R** multiple.
- A **$5,000 gain** = a **10R** multiple.
- If, unfortunately, we take a **$1,000 loss**, that's a **-2R** loss.

Talking in terms of R multiples standardizes our risk across the board — across markets, products, and positions. This standardization makes things much easier to work with in practice and for analysis purposes.

Remember: our primary job as traders should be to devise a plan that gets us profits that are large multiples of R. Going back to our initial discussion of the payoff ratio (1:2, 1:3, 1:4 — the higher the better), we also want our system to produce 1:2, 1:3 R multiples — the higher, the better.
