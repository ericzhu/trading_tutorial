# Module 5, Video 6: The Complete Money and Risk Management Rulebook

Let's jump into our initial risk management. The first step is making sure we operate within the constraints of allowed leverage factors we've discussed, and protect ourselves against unpredictable price gaps — we've just covered how destructive those can be to our accounts.

## Step 1: Know Your Overall Exposure and Leverage

Our task every day is to be up to date on our overall portfolio position and leverage. This can be very easy if you only have one active broker, or take a bit more time with multiple brokers — but no matter what, it absolutely needs to be done first thing, before markets open and before you put on any more positions.

You should also get in the habit of reviewing your overall personal balance sheet every quarter or so — understanding your overall position in cash, securities, real estate, currency exposure, outstanding liabilities, etc., so you have a decent sense of what proportion your trading account represents within your overall personal finances. This is a bit of a parenthesis, but it's important — this course is about trading and investing, but also about having an overall view of how you manage your assets and liabilities. If you're becoming an active trader/investor, you want to know how much money is dedicated to trading versus the grand scheme of your finances.

Remember: your trading account should only hold money you can afford to lose — money that doesn't put your lifestyle in danger. But that doesn't mean this is "gambling money" — please don't misunderstand me. Our task is preservation of capital: capital we've worked hard for, saved over many years. We don't want to endanger it. Trading is neither gambling, nor is it here to fulfill some need for excitement (we've covered that already, plenty). You need to treat your trading account very seriously and professionally, like any business — especially if your ultimate goal is to make trading an essential, income-generating part of your everyday life.

### Tracking Exposure with a Spreadsheet

The way I personally track this is with a spreadsheet, updated every night so my numbers and exposures are known to me before I start a fresh trading day. (A simple example spreadsheet will be made available to you — fine-tune it to your own needs and profile.)

In this spreadsheet, you review all your trading accounts and their current equity (cash + securities combined). For example, say you have three brokers:
- **Broker 1** — $20,000 deposited
- **Broker 2** — $30,000 deposited
- **Broker 3** — $50,000 deposited

For each broker, list every open position and its **nominal exposure** — the true notional size of the position, not just the margin used. For example, if Broker 1 is your FX broker, list your EUR/USD position, GBP/JPY position, gold position, etc. (whether futures, CFDs, or spread betting) and calculate the exact nominal exposure of each. Do the same for Broker 2, and for Broker 3 (say, your stock broker) — if you have three stock positions worth $20,000 each outstanding.

Once you've listed every position and its nominal exposure, add them up per broker and as a grand total. In this example: total equity across the three brokers = $100,000, but total nominal exposure = $214,000 — giving a total leverage ratio of about **2.14x**. This is simply: total nominal exposure across every position, divided by total equity.

**Important note:** the nominal exposure you include should only cover positions that still carry a negative (loss-risking) stop loss. Any position whose stop has already been moved to breakeven should not be counted in the nominal position size — because that trade can no longer turn negative... in theory. In practice, a large enough price gap can pierce through a stop-loss order and trigger a loss on an otherwise "protected" breakeven position. It happens. That said, for FX and commodities, gaps (especially overnight or news-driven ones) tend to be relatively limited compared to equities. For equities, I'll factor the possibility of a price gap into overall position sizing when trading single stocks — we'll cover that later. Also keep in mind that most CFD/spread-betting brokers offer **guaranteed stop losses** (a bit more expensive) that close your trade at exactly the price you specify, no matter the volatility or gap — the broker eats the difference, not you.

### Leverage Limits by Experience Level

Having calculated my own accounts sit at just over 2x leverage, here are the leverage guidelines I recommend:

- **Beginners:** no leverage at all to start — at least for your first year of trading while learning the method and system.
- **Intermediate traders** (roughly 2-4 years of experience): a limited leverage of **1:3**.
- **Advanced traders** (5+ years of experience): up to **3-5x** leverage.

Some of you might think this sounds very conservative compared to what you've heard elsewhere. This is my strong recommendation, based on experience — not just my own, but watching many people, professional and non-professional, consistently underestimate the issue of leverage and risk, and take on more of it simply because brokers (especially FX, commodity, and futures brokers) will offer virtually unlimited leverage. You have to be very careful — that's the entire purpose of this module and this system. Putting on trades too large for your account can be deadly.

What I've noticed over the years: beginner traders hurry to make money, concentrating on how much they'll make and how they'll spend it. The opposite happens with pro traders — they start by assessing how much they potentially stand to lose; that's their number one priority ("What can go wrong, and if it does, how much?"). For them, making money is the cherry on the cake — it comes naturally once they've protected their account and preserved capital. As we said earlier, it's better to start small, focus on process and quality of trades, and build up from there. This risk and money management system allows exactly that: it lets you increase your trading size the right way, and all it takes is the discipline to stick to the rules.

## Step 2: Initial Risk Guidelines Per Trade

Now, for the next trade I'm about to take, here are the initial risk parameters to stick to:

- **Single stocks**: initial risk of **0.1%** of equity per trade, with a maximum nominal capital exposure of **5%** of total equity.
  - For a $50,000 account: max initial risk = **$50** per trade, and max nominal position size = **$5,000** (5% of equity) in that stock.
- **ETFs**: initial risk of **0.3%** per trade (ETFs are more liquid and diversified, so the risk is inherently less than trading single stocks), with a maximum nominal cap exposure of **10%** of total equity — so up to $5,000 on a $50,000 account.
- **FX or commodities**: initial risk of **0.1%** of equity per trade, with the maximum leverage ratio as suggested above, based on your experience level.

I know what you're thinking: "0.1%? That's nothing!" It is nothing, in the grand scheme of things — but there's a reason behind it, which we'll get to shortly.

## Step 3: Daily, Weekly, and Monthly Max Loss Limits

Now we need rules to manage risk on an ongoing basis — not just per trade. What's the difference between someone like you or me and a hedge fund manager? I've seen cases of hedge fund managers, or proprietary bank traders, who were very successful in an institutional setting, making a lot of money — but when they go off on their own as independent proprietary traders, they suddenly can't make money anymore, and they wonder why.

Here's why: a big part of why they made money institutionally was that there was a risk manager behind them — someone whose job was to ensure the overall exposure of the bank/fund and its traders made sense, controlling it very strictly. Each trader had a maximum loss they could take per day, week, or month. Go over it, and they're pulled off — capital reduced, forced to take a break, or shown the door. That's why banks and hedge funds survive with so many portfolio managers: risk is controlled both individually and at the portfolio level. Once someone leaves that institutional system and is on their own, they sometimes lack the discipline to apply that control themselves — which is why they can no longer manage their losses, an essential part of trading.

So here are the rules, for **single stocks, FX, and commodities**, based on the 0.1% initial risk per trade:

- **Daily max loss: 0.3%** — meaning you could have, say, three single-stock trades open at once; if you lose on all three in a row, you must stop trading for the day.
- **Weekly max loss: 0.75%**
- **Monthly max loss: 2%**

**Example walkthrough:** Say on the first day of the week you lose three trades in a row (0.3%) and stop for the day. Second day, you start again, lose three in a row (another 0.3%), and stop again. Third day, halfway through, you've already lost 0.6% for the week — only 0.15% left before you must stop trading for the entire week.

If these risk limits are reached, trading must stop for the rest of that time unit under consideration. For example, if the weekly max loss is hit on a Wednesday, trading must immediately stop for the remainder of that week. Same logic applies monthly: if you're down more than 2% at any point in the month, stop trading real money for the rest of the month — go back to your strategy, trade a paper account, review your trading journal for mistakes. You're not quitting observing the market — just quitting trading your real account for that period.

## Step 4: The Drawdown Rule

There's one more important rule layered on top of all these max caps: the **drawdown rule**.

- If your total equity is in a drawdown of **10% or more**, reduce your trading size (the initial risk per trade) by **20%**.
- For every additional 10% drawdown beyond that, reduce trading size by another 20%.

A quick reminder of what "drawdown" means: it doesn't necessarily mean losing 10% relative to your original starting capital. It can happen two ways:
1. You start with $50,000 and lose immediately, down to $45,000 — a straightforward 10% drawdown from your initial deposit.
2. You start with $50,000, grow the account all the way up to $100,000, then lose back down to $90,000 — also a 10% drawdown, even though you're still up $40,000 overall from your starting stake.

The emotional experience of these two scenarios is very different, but the 10% drawdown rule applies regardless — whether you lose immediately or start losing back some of your gains, it's there to remind you: something might be off. Maybe the market isn't favorable for you right now, maybe your strategy isn't working at the moment, maybe your patterns aren't adapting to current conditions. Whatever the reason, you need to slow down and reduce your trade size — which is exactly what this rule forces you to do (reduce by 20%, then another 20% for every further 10% drawdown). This rule, layered on top of the max caps, acts like an outside risk manager for you.

## Sizing Risk to Your Own Psychology

When the level of risk goes up, our ability to perform goes down. It's very important not to trade a size that's too large from your own perspective — the "right" size isn't the same for everyone, and it must be adapted to each person's own psychology.

So while I recommend an initial risk per trade of 0.1%, applied to the overall size of your account, the nominal dollar amount at risk must also be psychologically and emotionally acceptable to you personally. For example: on a $50,000 account, 0.1% = $50. On a $1 million account (and yes, some of you may have accounts that size), 0.1% = $1,000 per trade. You need to be absolutely clear in your own mind whether you can live with risking $1,000 on every single trade — because some people get psychologically stuck on a nominal risk amount that feels too large for their comfort, even if the percentage is small and controlled.

So you have to understand yourself — what you're capable of living with comfortably. Percentages are the best way to think about this generally, and 0.1% truly is nothing in the grand scheme of things (and I'll show you it can still make a lot of money while limiting losses) — but if even 0.1% feels like too much on a nominal dollar basis for your account, lower the number. It doesn't matter — that's not an issue. You need to be comfortable, and you build up from there, slowly but surely.

## Simulation: How the System Performs

**Best case, using the same parameters throughout:** this simulation is based on **48 full weeks of trading per year** (assuming a few weeks off for holidays — you might trade more or fewer weeks depending on your habits).

- With a 60% win ratio, a **1:1 payoff ratio** (risking one, making one on winners, losing one on losers), the estimated annual return would be around **14%**. How can this be profitable at 1:1 payoff? Because of the edge from the win ratio — winning 6 out of 10 trades, losing only 4 — plus a subtlety: the actual average payoff on losing trades often ends up being **less than 1**, because in practice, a lot of the time our initial stop loss never gets triggered as originally set — we move it up, to breakeven, or elsewhere — so the average realized loss ends up being something like 70-80% of the originally intended risk. The same dynamic applies (in reverse) to the payoff on winning trades: even if we aim for a 1:2 or 1:3 risk-reward, the way we actually manage positions often means the realized average payoff ends up more like 1.5-2.5, rather than the full theoretical 3.
- With an **average payoff ratio of around 1.5** (realistic after accounting for how positions actually get managed), the annual return comes out around **40-50%** (examples of 49%, 46% shown in the table).
- Increasing the risk per trade from 0.1% to **0.5%** (five times the initial risk), keeping the same ~1.5 average payoff ratio, the annual return jumps to around **200-240%**.

**Worst case scenario:** Now let's flip it around. Suppose, every single day, we hit our max daily loss, then our max weekly loss, then our max monthly loss — repeating this, week after week, with a **0% win ratio** — the worst of the worst. (Realistically, I don't think anyone could psychologically or emotionally endure that for long, and I've never encountered a trader that bad — even poor traders usually manage a 20-40% win ratio. But let's assume the absolute worst case, continuously, for years.)

With the max daily/weekly/monthly loss caps in place, plus the drawdown-reduction rule (reducing size by 20% for every additional 10% drawdown), here's what happens to the cumulative loss (as a percentage of initial trading equity) over time:

- After about week 27: down roughly **10%** of the account.
- After about week 52 (one year): down roughly **20%**.
- After **240 weeks** (just under five years) of continuous, worst-possible losing: the system has still preserved close to **40%** of the original capital — meaning only about **60%** has been lost, despite five straight years of the worst conceivable trading performance.

This is entirely due to the risk and money management rules — the strict guardrails that protect us on the downside when we're losing. Even in the absolute worst case imaginable, it would take roughly **four years or a little more** to lose even 50% of your initial capital.

Do you see now how important it is to follow these money and risk management rules? Even if you turned out to be the worst trader imaginable, it would take you an enormous amount of time before you'd even lose most of your capital — giving you plenty of time to realize trading isn't for you and to try something else, all while never having "lost your shirt," only a very limited percentage of your initial capital.
