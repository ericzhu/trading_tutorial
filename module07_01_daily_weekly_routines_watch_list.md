# Module 7, Video 1: Daily and Weekly Routines — Getting Prepared and Building Your Watch List

Module 7 covers the daily and weekly routines needed to get properly prepared before the trading session opens, and how to build the list of instruments to trade.

## Step 1: Decide What to Trade

There are countless tradable instruments — stocks, commodities, ETFs, foreign exchange, and more. It's fine to start with a mixed bag and later narrow down to the markets that feel like the best fit.

## Step 2: Define What Type of Trader You Want to Be

This was covered in earlier modules, but it bears repeating: figure out early on what kind of trader/investor you want to become.
- **Active/day trader type:** needs regular action, in and out of positions frequently.
- **Active investor type:** checks the portfolio at least once or twice a day without needing to stay glued to a screen, generally ending up doing medium-to-long-term swing trades. Note this is deliberately called an "active investor," not the common but flawed idea that "investor" simply means buy-and-hold (which isn't really a strategy on its own) — an active investor is willing to turn over the portfolio to adjust to market conditions more often than not.

This choice directly determines **Screen #2** in the MTF convergence system, so it's crucial to figure out your desired activity level. There's no need to know the right answer from day one — it's fine to experiment with different timeframes and styles for a while before settling on what matches your personality. Everyone is different, and the goal of this course is to provide maximum guidance and methodology so you can become independent as quickly as possible — not to create dependency on any particular guru, including the instructor.

## Step 3: Start Small — No More Than 4 Instruments

When starting out (especially if new to trading), stick to a small, limited list of **no more than four instruments**:
1. One FX pair,
2. One specific sector/industry ETF (e.g., SPDR, Fidelity, or iShares sector ETFs),
3. One general index (Dow Jones, S&P, German DAX, etc.), tradable via ETF (SPY, DIA), CFD, or futures,
4. One commodity of interest (gold, silver, crude oil are the typical liquid, active choices).

CFDs are generally recommended to start with, given their liquidity and small tradable lot sizes, which make it easier to get familiar with each market.

### Advantages of Starting Small
- Avoids "chasing too many rabbits at once" and catching none.
- Doesn't require a massive time commitment to stay well-prepared, keeping the workload manageable.
- Leaves enough time to properly complete pre-market study, intervention levels, and the trading plan.
- Helps build familiarity with the trading rules and consistency, without being overwhelmed by too many candidates — while still maintaining a reasonably diversified starter portfolio, which may reveal a natural preference for a particular market over time.

One of the most important steps toward long-term trading success is getting organized and focused — narrowing down to the setups most likely to be profitable. Focus doesn't mean staring at a screen for seven hours; it means allocating time to what actually makes money.

## Choosing the Starter Portfolio: FX Example

Since volatility is the trader's friend (to be tamed through discipline and proper risk/money/position management), a good FX pair should offer better-than-average volatility. A useful starting tool is the **Forex Volatility Calculator** on Investing.com (found in the site's toolbox navigation menu). Stick to major currency pairs and avoid truly exotic ones (Turkish lira, South African rand, Russian ruble, etc.).

As an example from a past snapshot of the 10-week volatility calculation, pairs like **AUD/JPY**, **GBP/AUD**, and **GBP/NZD** offered notably better volatility than a "boring" pair like **EUR/USD** — even though EUR/USD is by far the most heavily traded pair in the world by sheer participant count.

This selection matters even more for active intraday traders, who are specifically looking for intraday volatility (while still able to convert an intraday position into a multi-day swing trade if warranted). As a day trader, though, positions are generally managed with the intent of being flat by the end of the trading day.

For the "active investor" category, a more detailed treatment of building a diversified ETF-based portfolio (covering close to the full spectrum of retail-accessible asset classes) will come later, with asset allocation shaped by your financial situation, age, risk aversion, etc.

## Widening the List Over Time

After training for a while on the reduced 4-instrument basket (and starting with a simulated/practice methodology), the candidate list can be widened — not to try to follow the entire universe of securities (humanly impossible), but to become a bit more versatile and responsive to what's actively moving week to week.

## Using Scanners to Manage a Larger, Rotating List

Scanners automate the process of finding the right candidates according to your own strategy criteria, far more efficiently than manual review. Consider the scale of the opportunity set: roughly 100+ FX pairs, ~25,000–30,000 stocks/ETFs in the US alone, 50 major commodities, plus a large number of bond futures, short-term interest rate products, etc. The list is effectively endless — this is where scanning software (a "robot" you configure yourself) becomes essential.

For FX specifically, you can either limit yourself to a very small number of pairs based on volatility (as described above), or use automatic scanners that look for trend-convergence and momentum criteria to stay versatile and reactive week to week. These scanners are available in most major charting platforms (e.g., TC2000, ProRealTime, MetaStock, TradingView) as well as many broker execution platforms.

For stock trading specifically, scanners are essentially mandatory given the sheer number of US securities — the main tools used are **TC2000** and **Trade Ideas**, for both intraday and swing candidate selection.

### General Scanner Criteria (not mutually exclusive)
1. **Unusual high relative volume (RVOL)** — e.g., a stock averaging 1 million shares/day over the past 20 days/month that's projected to trade 4×, 5×, or 10× that average on a given day.
2. **Multi-day breakouts** — tied to the core breakout strategies covered previously.
3. **Reversal warning signals** — tied to the MRA (Major Reversal Warning) strategy.
4. **Convergence of price and EMAs** — tied to the B2B strategy.
5. **Strong news catalysts** — a significant plus when backing the price action seen on a given day.

TC2000 scanners are used for breakout, reversal, and pullback (B2B) patterns for both swing and intraday trades; Trade Ideas scanners are used for things like top gainers/losers of the day, volatility/price breakouts, and opening gappers, feeding into a weekly target list. (Note: proprietary scanners are made available to course members.)

## The Daily Morning Preparation Routine

Trading is a business and must be treated as such — if trading is just for fun/excitement, that money is better spent at a casino, where at least the loss comes with some entertainment. The morning routine below is a personal checklist — much like a pilot's pre-flight checklist and flight-route planner — for navigating the competitive arena of the financial markets:

1. **Open the main broker platform and keep a backup device ready** — a phone, tablet, or second laptop hooked to a 4G connection in case Wi-Fi/internet access fails, so you can still exit or monitor positions urgently without being cut off from the market for long.
2. **Open the charting software platform.**
3. **Open the scanning platform** (e.g., TC2000, Trade Ideas, or any other scanning software).
4. **Open the risk/position-sizing spreadsheet** and check overall open positions, nominal size, and confirm you're within the leverage/risk management guidelines from Module 5 (Step 1 of risk management). This spreadsheet (included with the Module 5 materials) tracks overall leverage per trading account and should stay open all day, since it also includes tabs for quick position-sizing calculations across stocks, CFDs, cryptos, etc.
5. **Open Snagit (or any screen-capture app)** — used constantly to capture chart screenshots right before taking a trade, and sometimes right after closing partial profits; these screenshots are integrated into the trading journal. Screen recording is sometimes used too, for reviewing live action later (covered further in Module 8, on getting organized).
6. **Review the news** — main sources: Reuters, BBC, Bloomberg, Investing.com, Benzinga, MarketWatch, etc. (not an exhaustive list, just the main anchor sites used).
7. **Check Twitter (via a dedicated Twitter-following tool)** — used to concentrate all important tweets from followed fellow traders and roughly 30 major news channels in one feed, making it rare to miss important general news.
8. **Review the calendar of major economic releases** (via Investing.com) — checking release times and major meetings (G7, Fed, ECB, BOE, etc.), filterable by importance (1–3 "bulls," 3 being most important) and by country.
9. **Review the earnings calendar** (same Investing.com source) — checking which major companies report earnings that day (pre-market or post-market close), and how actual results compared to expectations. Since trading here is technically/chart-driven, fundamental data (economic stats, earnings) is used mainly to know when to expect potential market disturbances — as a rule, avoid opening new positions right ahead of a major economic report (like Non-Farm Payrolls, released the first Friday of each month) or ahead of a company's earnings report, unless there's a very strong conviction, or unless adjusting/hedging an existing position that could be adversely affected. This is subjective — some traders are comfortable holding through this volatility — but the general recommendation is not to gamble blindly into these events, since (barring insider information, which is not recommended) the odds are generally stacked against you due to genuine surprise risk.
10. **Review upcoming IPOs** (again via the Investing.com calendar) — some IPOs are brand-new companies going public, others are essentially refinancings.
11. **Take the "morning temperature" of the market** — review the closes of major US, Asian, and European benchmark indices. An across-the-board "green" morning suggests the next market to open (and whatever you're trading) may be influenced by that positive ambient tone — a useful (though not guaranteed) initial trend-setter, since the mood can shift on unexpected news (e.g., surprise tweets or a bad economic report).
12. **Review the watch list and intervention levels** — after getting a general read on the US market via the major indices (SPY, QQQ, IWM/Russell, etc.), review the pre-planned "A list": specific price levels for intervention, price alerts already set for potential entries of interest, etc.

There is absolutely no excuse for being unprepared. The idea of waking up ten minutes before the open ready to trade effectively is pure fantasy — a minimum amount of mental preparation is required before facing live market conditions. Once in the middle of live action, emotional involvement makes clear thinking and good decisions much harder. Treat trading like a flight plan: know in advance what to do if the market goes up, down, or does nothing. With everything pre-planned and crystal clear, you remove emotional interference and can act decisively — almost on autopilot — without needing anyone else's opinion. The same logic applies elsewhere in life: no one would walk into a job interview, an important business negotiation, or a final exam without proper preparation — trading is no different; it can't be done on impulse alone.

13. **Run the usual scanners to identify any interesting additions to the watch list** — only after covering the "A list" is it time to check current-day movers and run the usual scans to see if there are 2–3 additional names worth watching for the day or the days ahead. This typically includes checking overnight/pre-market movers via a source like Benzinga (a strong US-stocks-focused news site) and running the Trade Ideas pre-market gappers scanner.

## A Personal Note on "Less Is More"

A strong personal preference for focusing on a short list of names, rather than following everything that moves, tends to be more profitable over the long run. This wasn't always the case — an earlier background trading in the style of large macro hedge funds (in the mold of Paul Tudor Jones, George Soros, Louis Bacon) favored a "the more markets, the merrier" approach, tracking essentially anything tradable. Over time it became clear that those large funds have the resources and manpower to properly track that many markets, whereas an individual trader working alone needs to stay focused on a limited list — some very successful traders build their entire career around trading just one instrument, while others thrive juggling many. It's a genuinely personal choice.

That said, a lingering "macro" mindset made it hard to cut the tradable list down below three broad asset classes: bonds, stocks, and commodities — these became the practical minimum kept under active watch, while still keeping the overall list manageable. The next part of this module walks through the actual selection process used, purely as one example among many valid approaches — the goal is to try different approaches until you land on the one that feels right for you.
