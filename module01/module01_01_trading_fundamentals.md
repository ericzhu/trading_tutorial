## Bid and Ask Spread

To start with, I'd like to go back to an essential but important notion: the bid and ask spread. What's the bid and ask? Most of you will already know this, so feel free to skip ahead a minute or two. But for those who don't, it's important to understand.

The bid and ask represent the price at which buyers and sellers meet. The bid is the price a buyer is willing to pay for a stock or any financial instrument. The ask is the price a seller is willing to sell it at. The difference between the two is what we call the bid-ask spread. Naturally, the more liquid the instrument, the smaller the spread will be, since there are plenty of buyers and sellers to accommodate one another.

I've got an example here of a bid-ask spread, and you'll notice this spread can evolve over time. In this case, we're looking at the US dollar against the Japanese yen: the bid price is 107.63 and the ask price is 107.64½. That tiny difference of 0.015 is called "one and a half pips" — a specific term used in foreign exchange.

## Why We Focus on US Stocks

Our focus will be on stock trading, including ETFs, on US stock exchanges. The three major American exchanges are the NYSE (New York Stock Exchange), NASDAQ, and AMEX — although AMEX was taken over by NYSE Euronext in 2008.

You might ask why we focus on the US. I'll tell you why: the bulk of global volume and liquidity is still concentrated there. Let's look at some statistics. This table shows the major world stock exchanges compared to each other as of April 2018. The two major US exchanges, NYSE and NASDAQ, clearly post the highest market cap compared to the rest of the top 10. Similarly, when comparing monthly trading volumes in nominal terms, the major liquidity is again US-based.

The same holds for stock options trading — the Americas, of which the US makes up 80–85% of the volume, hold a near-monopoly compared to other major global exchanges. And finally, the ETF market is king in terms of liquidity in the Americas. The numbers show a staggering difference: even though the number of ETFs listed in the US is only half of what's listed in EMEA (Europe, Middle East, and Africa), the trading volume gap is still remarkable.

I won't dwell any longer on these statistics, but I wanted to make the point as to why we favor the US market for trading stocks. As you've seen, the US offers the most liquidity and the widest choice of listed securities from around the world.

That said, if you're set on trading your local exchange because you're more familiar with the companies, or for whatever reason, I won't stand in your way — but I'll tell you this: first, it never pays to have a home bias when it comes to trading or investing. Second, I've traded many stocks in France, Germany, the UK, Greece — you name it — and I was rarely happy with the liquidity. That's why I always came back to the US market and the thousands of listed securities and opportunities it offers every day.

Another feature I like as a Europe-based trader is that US market hours work well for me. The market opens at 4:30pm my local time, which gives me plenty of time to run my scans and analyses and prepare before the market opens, while still trading some of my favorite forex pairs and commodities earlier in the day.

It's also worth noting that, because of the time difference, European traders can keep their 9-to-5 job and still trade part of the US morning session, or part of the rest of the US trading day. This kind of setup helps people get their feet wet in trading while keeping their current job and income.

Now I'd like to talk about another important parameter in stock trading: tickers. Stocks are abbreviated using three- or four-letter codes called tickers. For example, Microsoft trades on the NYSE under the ticker MSFT, and JPMorgan Chase trades under JPM. ## Trading Hours

Now, back to important details about the US stock exchanges: the operating hours.

- **Pre-market**: opens at 7:00am Eastern time until 9:30am. This is about two and a half hours of unofficial trading. Liquidity is usually much lower, but some traders still manage to place positions before the official market hours begin.
- **Official market hours**: 9:30am to 4:00pm Eastern time. This is where you get the most volume, especially in the first hour or two of trading (9:30–11:30am Eastern), which is when the major bulk of volume happens for US stocks.
- **Post-market**: after the market closes at 4:00pm (1600), there's about an hour of post-market trading, though liquidity dries up considerably.

We generally don't recommend trading in the pre-market or post-market sessions. Sometimes a stock reacts to breaking news or an announcement outside these hours and you'll see a lot of liquidity build up in the pre-market — you might dabble in that if you want, but my advice is to wait for official market hours starting at 9:30am US time.

## Foreign Exchange (Forex/FX)

The foreign exchange market, commonly called Forex or FX, is the largest and by far the most liquid market traded on a global scale. According to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), foreign exchange trading averages $5.3 trillion a day, with the US dollar making up the majority of Forex transactions. Comparing Forex volume to other major markets really shows just how huge and liquid it is.

Let me show you how an FX platform works. Here's the platform of a broker called FXCM, which is strong in foreign exchange, commodities, and some indices. On this screen, you'll see the symbol format for FX: the first three letters represent one currency, and the second three letters represent the other. For example, GBP/AUD is the Australian dollar per British pound, GBP/NZD is the New Zealand dollar per British pound, and USD/TRY is the Turkish lira per US dollar. You start to get the pattern.

There's also "US Oil," which on the FXCM platform represents the NYMEX futures contract on crude oil, and XAU/USD, a common symbol used across many platforms for gold.

For FX, another important factor is the rollover swap rate. When you hold a position in one currency versus another overnight, the interest rate differential between the two currencies creates a swap rate — a cost or a credit, depending on the position. For example, with NZD/USD (the number of US dollars per one New Zealand dollar): if I buy the currency and hold the position overnight, I get charged a negative rollover, which costs me money. If I sell the New Zealand dollar and buy the US dollar instead, I get credited a small rollover swap amount each day, meaning that position actually earns me money over time.

This is a typical CFD (Contract for Difference) platform. Why do I show you FXCM specifically? There are plenty of brokers out there, but I like FXCM because it was once one of the first big companies in this space. It had to downsize after the Swiss franc crisis in 2015, but it remains a well-capitalized company. Importantly, they also offer micro lots — typically one-tenth the minimum size other platforms require. I like platforms that support smaller trade sizes because it lets students trade with real (rather than paper) accounts, even with small balances — where every point of movement might represent just 10 cents.

## Brokers

Now that I've shown you FXCM, let's talk a bit about brokers. Brokers are the intermediaries who act on our behalf when we want to buy or sell any financial instrument. They're part of the chain required to transact any financial instrument. Most of the time, they earn money either through a set commission on the amount transacted, or through the bid-ask spread itself, since they may act as market makers on their platform.

Brokers come in all shapes, sizes, and nationalities. On screen, you can see FXCM in the top-left corner (a platform built on top of Interactive Brokers, a major industry player), along with IG Group (a big UK group focused mostly on CFDs and spread betting), LMAX Exchange (which we'll cover shortly), and some well-known US stockbrokers at the bottom like Lightspeed, SureTrader, and E*Trade.

One piece of advice: always go with a well-capitalized, well-known, main-street brand name for your broker.
