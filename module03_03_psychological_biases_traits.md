# Module 3, Video 3: Psychological Biases and Character Traits to Overcome

Welcome to video number three, part of Module 3 on psychology. Earlier we talked about how important it is to understand psychological biases and weaknesses and strive to work on them. If we go back to the 14 emotional stages of the investing cycle, we realize how some character traits can be bad for our trading, and how they should be recognized and addressed accordingly. So I'd like to cover some of these bad traits that we should strive to get rid of.

## Hope

Hope is a beautiful word, but every time you hear yourself talking about a position saying something along the lines of "I hope the market does this," or "hopefully," or "fingers crossed" — this is a sign of trouble. You're probably already in a state of denial without even knowing it. So be careful — every time you hear yourself saying these sort of things, pay attention.

## Stubbornness

Refusing to admit that you're wrong — you need to be able to change your opinion as the market changes. It's very important that you don't get married to any position you might hold. If it's not working, let it go; if it is working, keep it until it's not working. Simple as that. Don't be stubborn — be adaptable. Adapt your trading, your mentality, to the market you're in and to what you see the market offering you at the moment.

## Greed

Most of you have probably remembered the famous line from Gordon Gekko in Oliver Stone's *Wall Street*: "Greed is good." Well, greed is not the best thing for our trading. Trends don't last forever, and there's a time to get out, because otherwise you can end up giving back much of what you've made by overstaying.

This also applies when you want the market to do something for you — for example, you want to make enough money to buy a new car you really like, and you suddenly start looking at the market as a source of cash rather than a place of trading opportunities. That's when you're trying to squeeze more out of the market than it's willing to give you. You won't exit the position until you hit your money objective — to pay for the car, the fridge, whatever it is you had in mind. Meanwhile, the market starts reversing, and a good trade can turn into a loser simply because you become obstinate and think you can't afford the loss. This trade could have been a good trade, and if you had traded the market instead of trading your dream profit, you would have made money. So the conclusion is: never get hung up on the number. Never get hung up on any specific project you might have in mind — like buying something — when you know you want to get out and won't get your price. Just get out. The real money is not made by squeezing out the last cents or catching the top or the bottom, as we've discussed before — the real money is made by catching the middle, what we call the meat of the move. Also keep in mind that greed often leads to oversizing the position — over-leveraging your account — which is absolutely forbidden in our book.

## Fear

The next characteristic is a very important one: fear. Fear as it relates to trading can take many different forms. Let's look at the most common types of fear you might experience, from the start of your trading career to later on.

**Fear of pulling the trigger.** Sometimes people are just too scared to put on a trade, even though they have good knowledge and a good methodology — for one reason or another, they don't want to, or can't, pull the trigger; they're afraid. I've always found that to beat this "freeze syndrome," as I like to call it, you need to gain more confidence in yourself and in your methodology. I've never found any better way than to put on some trades that are so insignificant in terms of monetary value to you — and again, this has to relate to what it means to you, and you only — that I like to call these "Mickey Mouse trades." They won't raise any fear of loss or bad feelings, so they should engage you to go ahead and take the trade. You won't be afraid anymore, because the amount of money on the line is something you can accept — you've come to terms with your own psychology and personality, and you're okay with taking the trade. That's one solution to get over this fear of pulling the trigger.

**Fear of accepting the possibility of loss.** This somewhat relates to the first fear. Here, the trader is okay — he's gotten over the initial fear of pulling the trigger, and part of the psychological approach of being okay with putting on the trade. But he still has deep issues with accepting a potential loss, even though any trade has roughly a 50/50 chance of success or failure. If you start with this fear of not being able to accept the possibility of loss, you'll do everything to really limit the loss — and what this usually leads to is people setting stop losses that are way too tight for the market they're trading, getting stopped out of a trade when in fact the trade idea and the strategy were both good. They get stopped out because they're limiting themselves to a loss they're happy with. This is a situation where it's important to realize that risk management and money management will also help you tackle this issue.

**Fear of giving back profits.** Now you've got the trader who's gotten over the fear of pulling the trigger, and is accepting the possibility of loss, but now finds himself in a position that's making money. We discussed earlier about trying to cut losses short and let winners run — well, typically you might find yourself too scared to give back your paper profits, and you tend to get out of a position too early. We'll talk about how to tackle this in the money management module, but this too is part of the emotional balance in trading that you need to keep in check.

**FOMO (fear of missing out).** Let me tell you something very basic but very, very important: it is okay to miss a move. If you miss one trade, there will be another one right behind it. If you miss the start of the move and feel it's already too late, that's also okay, because you can always try to get in on the first pullback and get a chunk of the move, as opposed to getting the whole thing — but waiting for the right pullback or opportunity gives you the edge and a higher probability of success. Remember what we said about paying attention to our entries, waiting for the market to settle after a move or breakout, rather than chasing it, thinking "this is the one, if I miss this one that'll be the end of my career." I mean, seriously, you'll make that stupid move many times — I did it, everyone does at the beginning, definitely. And you'll soon realize how much discipline you need to wait for your turn to get in. But believe me — trains, you'll see many of them, like when you wait at a train station: you miss one, there's always another right after that.

Now the problem comes when you cannot resist — you think this is the trade of the year, and if you miss it, you'll regret it for the rest of your life. So you go ahead and take the trade — most of the time, obviously, the market is already very overextended. The correct placement of your stop becomes tricky, if not altogether bad, so your risk-to-reward ratio becomes really average. And despite all these warning signals, you still get in. So what often happens is that you buy close to, if not at, the top of the market. You get stopped out very quickly and start feeling frustrated, so you go back in — same stock, or perhaps a different one, but with the same FOMO approach — and you get stopped out again. This time the frustration really gets the better of your ego, and little by little you start losing your self-control, slowly but surely, because now you've become too emotionally clouded by all these losses. So you start trading angry.

That, my friend, is the start of a combination of bad composure. You're engaging in what we call revenge trading — thinking the market is out there to get you, that they want to take you out, and "I'll show them who's the boss." All these negative feelings push you into doing the wrong things, taking the wrong trades simply to prove a point — you don't even know to whom, but it doesn't really matter, because you need to vent your anger, and you're not even thinking straight anymore. The only way you can find, right here and right now, is to take your anger out on the market, which at the end of the day is totally impersonal, as we said — it couldn't care less about who you are, where you live, your losses, etc. It doesn't care, it doesn't matter. Many, many traders have burned their accounts trading angry. As this slide's quote from master trader Ed Seykota says: "Trying to trade during a losing streak is emotionally devastating. Trying to play catch-up is lethal." This sentence sums it all up. You can't trade hungry — you have to get your ego out of the way, you have to be in control, otherwise the rest will be in control of you, and that's when trouble happens.

So how do you deal with these situations, and what should you do to ringfence your emotions and trading from this type of destructive behavior?

## Techniques for Regaining Control

We'll study the techniques and rules of strict risk management in Module 5, but as a teaser: you need to set yourself a max daily loss cap. To do that realistically, you need to determine what the real maximum loss is that you're ready to accept — and only you can figure out this number that feels comfortable and right to you.

Here are the other ways a trader can control himself and clear his mind when he falls into these bad character traits:

**First, go back to your trading plan, your methodology, your strategy, and make sure you're following it.** Review your plan, and if everything checks out — you've done what you were supposed to do — that's the first and most important step.

**Second, if you're close to your stop loss, or have already gone through it, take the loss and move on, in order to look at things from a fresh perspective.** I remember a chapter in the book *Pit Bull*, about Marty Schwartz, where he describes being in such a position — I think around some kind of US election — with a position that had gone the wrong way. He was down more than a million bucks, wasn't sleeping at night, and didn't know what to do. He calls up his mentor and explains the situation, saying the market can't be like that, he's sure he's right. And the mentor basically tells him: "Look, Marty, you've got to take the loss, you've got to take the stop loss. Just take it, and you'll have plenty of occasions to recoup your losses — you've shown yourself you can make money consistently, almost every day, and this time won't be any different. Maybe you over-leveraged, maybe you overstayed, maybe your ego took control, but you need to take that loss." At the end of the day, Marty Schwartz listens to him, and when the market opens in the morning, closes all his positions and suddenly feels released — he's taken the weight off his shoulders and feels this was the right thing to do. The proof was in the pudding: before the end of the year, maybe a month later, he had recouped all his losses and more. But the idea is that you need to take the loss and move on — you need to be psychologically clear in order to start looking at things from a fresh perspective.

**Take a forced break.** Go out, take a walk, go to the gym, go shopping with your wife or girlfriend, whoever it is — but take a break from the market, take a break from your screen. You'll come back refreshed, with a lot less emotion clouding your mind, and you'll be able to start reviewing things from a fresh perspective.

**Consider therapy — or hypnosis.** It has worked on many people, myself included; I did it in the past. Some people are afraid of hypnosis, but honestly there's nothing to be afraid of. As we discussed with Robert Kraus, who was a trader and also a specialist in hypnotherapy, there are ways to go deep down into your subconscious and try to line it up with your conscious mind to help you in your trading — not only your trading. A lot of people use hypnotherapy to stop smoking, to diet, etc. It doesn't work every time, and it doesn't work for everyone, but the fact is that it works in a lot of cases. So you've got nothing to lose — just a few bucks to book a meeting with a hypnotherapist. Having two, three, four sessions and seeing how it goes might help you — it definitely helped me.

**And last but not least, hire a trading coach** — which you did, and you made the right choice.

## Overconfidence

Finally, I'd like to talk about the last character trait you can find in your trading: overconfidence. We just saw how fear can impede our performance, but the other side of the coin is that overconfidence can also be a problem. In that case, we're making money and start losing perspective.

Overconfidence is a trait you can experience right from the start, with beginner's luck — even before you start trading, when you're a total newbie, you might also be overconfident, because everybody thinks they'll do great no matter what. That's the first thing you need to be aware of.

The bigger problem usually comes when you've been trading for a little while, and after a period of good winning, you start feeling invincible. You start thinking, "I've figured it all out, and everything I touch is going to turn to gold." So you get cocky, which leads you to put on average trades instead of waiting for the good ones — you get sloppy and stop doing your homework the way you should. From my experience, our worst setbacks come after good periods of winning, simply as a result of the lack of discipline that comes with newly gained overconfidence.

It's never a bad idea, in my view, to take a break after a few consistent days of winning — almost like a forced vacation, to reward yourself for your good work. By doing that, the interesting thing is that we're forcing our brain to come back down to earth a bit — we're not so carried away by what just happened, and we're stepping back before reaching that state of euphoria we saw is so dangerous in this investing and trading cycle, which could lead us into this type of overconfidence.

## Should You Set Yourself Money Targets?

Last but not least, a question that comes back to me very often from beginner traders: is it worth setting yourself money objectives? By money objectives, I don't necessarily mean a certain dollar amount per week or per day — it could also be targets in terms of pips, if you trade forex, or points, if it's stocks. What about setting yourself targets or objectives on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis?

My answer, most of the time, is that I personally don't advise setting yourself such monthly, weekly, or daily targets. Not setting yourself such targets enables you to do the following:

**First, it avoids the unnecessary stress and pressure of trying to reach that target.** If traders start thinking, "I need to make $200 every day," or "$1,000 every week," whatever the target is, they'll start feeling the pressure every day, coming into the market thinking "this is what I need to do — I've done it, I've done half of it, I haven't done it, I've lost money, what am I going to do?" This basically puts you in a bad spot from the start and creates unnecessary stress or pressure.

**Second, you have no limits, and therefore you're able to exploit the maximum a trade can give you.** By not setting yourself any targets, you come in with a plan according to your methodology, applying risk and money management, which lets you get involved in a trade, take some profits when they come along, but still manage to trade along with the trend and stay in it as much as we can. We'll cover that aspect in the money management and risk management module later on.

**Finally, and probably most importantly, you're not forcing any trade just because you need to reach a target.** Sometimes the market offers no opportunity at all — the market can be choppy, illiquid, or there might be some reason there's simply nothing to do, and you might not trade for one, two, three days, maybe a week, who knows. Usually the market offers plenty of opportunities, but sometimes it's better to stay out — sometimes the conditions don't correspond to your style of trading, and you won't find the right opportunities according to yourself. So why force trades when you can't find ones that suit your methodology and your personality? There's no reason to do that. And this brings us back in a circle to point number one — putting yourself in a bad spot and unnecessary stress, when there shouldn't be any reason for it.

That said, I'd like to tell you something: as much as I don't think setting yourself money objectives is a good idea, I think it is extremely important — vital, even — to set yourself strict terms and targets in terms of risk management. This is a must; this is vital to your survival as a trader. So if I were to impose one objective on you, it would be to follow strictly the terms and targets of your risk and money management.

To go back to what was mentioned before: your first priority is to apply patience, practice the methodology, and respect your trading plan and your risk and money management for a minimum period of six months. Do that religiously, every day, day in and day out. If you can set yourself to apply everything you've learned in this course from A to Z, you will gain confidence, you'll be able to operate without any stress whatsoever, and you'll see the results in your equity account and in your trading flourish in ways you haven't even been able to imagine before.
